## Kalaignar's Legislative Assembly Speeches on the Financial Statement

**Speech: 6**  
**Date: 7.3.1963**

**Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi:** Mr. Chairman, I wish to express my views on this year's Budget. I think it is better to say that I welcome and support this Budget. The reason is that even if one begins by saying that one opposes it and then rises to point out shortcomings, the members of the ruling party begin opposing that. If one says at the outset that one supports it and then points out a thousand shortcomings, they still take it in this House as support. Therefore, I think it is better to say that I welcome and support it. (**Hon. Mr. M. Bhaktavatsalam:** At the end, say that you reject it.)

I would also like to mention another reason for welcoming it. Even after some thirteen or fourteen years, however insistently those in the Opposition make their points about a Budget of this kind, and whatever arguments of policy they place before this august House, the rulers reject all of them and prepare and present the Budget as they please. For that reason, the people of the country today will understand the harshness of the rulers and realise the mistake they made. In that sense, one could even say rather wryly that I welcome it.

### Source page 61

Many different views have been expressed in this august House, from both sides, about this Budget. The sentence that the Finance Minister has included in the concluding part of the Budget statement is extremely important. The Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam does not deny that it is capable of touching the heart of every citizen of this country.

The Finance Minister has stated in the Budget: “There is no contradiction between the two objectives of defence and development. But if both are to be achieved together, we must mobilise all our manpower and material resources. If, in doing so, sacrifices have to be made, we must not, as citizens of a free country, shrink from them.” So far as we are concerned, there can certainly be no difference of opinion on this. At the very moment when India raised the banner of resistance against the aggression being carried out by the Chinese on India's northern frontier, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam set aside even its own objective and resolved, in all sincerity, to extend its support to the Government of India in this struggle. I wish to point out with great pride that, without retreating even by an iota from that decision, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam has been working in the conviction that defeating the Chinese aggression is our foremost task. This is a matter of life and death for every citizen of this country. How much concern we should show in that matter. How prepared we should be to make sacrifices is what, in this statement,

### Source page 62

the Finance Minister has stated, and that is worthy of praise.

But when we reflect on this a little, I wish to ask: “Must those who make the sacrifices be only the poor? Should the favourable and adverse effects arising from this war not touch the capitalist class in this country? Should they not reach the doorsteps of the wealthy?” Resources are needed for the country's development. Ways must indeed be found to raise those resources and increase revenue. But while finding those ways, should the entire burden fall only on the head of the poor citizen of this country? Every member belonging to the ruling party has spoken of this as though to say, ‘Is this not a Budget without taxes?’ Not only that. They said: “Because the Finance Minister has presented a Budget without imposing taxes, those in the Opposition have been left with no opportunity even to speak; therefore they are ‘thinking of aval and pounding the mortar’.” Whatever else all those members who spoke may have failed to say, they did not fail to repeat that proverb. I wish to mention what the respected Mrs. Ananthanayaki, who spoke yesterday, said. She said, ‘If we had imposed a tax in this Budget, that would have been the last tax.’ What can that sentence mean? It only shows that every tax that could be imposed has already been imposed and that there is nothing left to tax. Four or five days ago, we passed the water-tax and land-tax Bill. The burden of those taxes

### Source page 63

has fallen on the head of the poor citizen. The Central Government's tax measures are separately affecting the citizens of this country. I wish to ask, ‘Where is there any room to impose a tax in this year's Budget?’ Just as one may say that there is not even a place to insert a needle on the body of a person afflicted with smallpox, every kind of tax has now been imposed and the people of this country are in great distress. To those in the ruling party who say, ‘Had a tax been imposed, the Opposition would have had an opportunity to speak; because no tax has been imposed, their mouths have been shut,’ I wish to cite the words in which the Hon. Mada Gowder opposed a tax in this House. On 25 July 1957, an amendment Bill relating to plantation income tax came before the House.

All of us in the Opposition expressed our support for raising that tax. Even Mr. Chinnadurai, who was then in this House, said that there would be nothing wrong in raising the tax from 45 paise to 50 paise. I remember the words spoken at that time by our friend Mada Gowder: “If our industry and plantations are to advance in a healthy manner, they too must be given encouragement. Only if they are encouraged will they cultivate those crops well. By exporting the produce to foreign countries, we may also be able to earn foreign exchange to some extent. I strongly condemn imposing, in one stroke, a heavy tax on this important industry.” Our friend Mada Gowder spoke in this House in those words on 25 July. Whether we in the Opposition belong to the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam or to any other progressive opposition party,

### Source page 64

we have opposed only the tax burden imposed on those who cannot bear it; we do not object to taxes imposed on those who are able to bear them. I wish to make that clear here.

When it is said that there is ‘no tax at all’ in this Budget, what else is there in it to give us joy? There was a husband who beat and battered his wife every day. One day he told her, ‘Today I am not going to beat you.’ The wife asked, ‘Have you suddenly developed so much love for me?’ “No! No! My hand has begun to ache from beating you so much,” he replied. In the same way, because every possible tax has already been imposed to the point where there is no room left to impose another one, no new taxes could be imposed in this Budget. The people who are suffering in the country continue to suffer. Those people who are bearing the tax burden continue to sigh sadly, asking, ‘Will the burden not be lifted?’

When the Russian leader Khrushchev visited India, he put it very beautifully:

“The foremost right of man that can guarantee freedom is

the right to work, to secure a safe life today and tomorrow, and to be free from the terrible fears of unemployment and poverty.

For man to be freed from the exploitation of those who hold in their hands all the means of production—factories, mills, banks, houses, land and natural resources—and grow fat on them,

### Source page 65

is the highest form of individual freedom,” he said.

Is the Budget in this country being drawn up on this basis? Is it enough to depend only on taxation to increase revenue? Why does this Government refuse to consider the view we have put forward that industries should be nationalised? If one reads this year's Budget and examines the account of the private and public sectors, in the public sector there are the Neyveli project, the Guindy teleprinter factory, the Nilgiri raw-film factory, the heavy-boiler factory and the defence-equipment factory near Avadi. But in the private sector there are the alloy-steel factory in Salem district, two factories for manufacturing machinery for sugar mills, the Dharangadhara establishment producing soda ash and chemicals, (for that very purpose) the Mettur Chemical establishment, the Ennore fertiliser factory, the Cuddalore fertiliser factory, the Coimbatore fertiliser factory, the Parry establishment's ammonium-phosphate factory, the Seshashayee paper factory, the Sankagiri Durg cement factory, the Koor cement factory and the Viscose establishment's rayon factory. To this extent the private sector is being expanded. Where is the Avadi socialism in this? We must consider whether this kind of mixed socialism can bring light into the life of the poor citizen. When we say that bus transport, from which private capitalists are earning enormous profits, should be nationalised, why is there hesitation? There are more than 200 motor magnates in this country operating roughly five thousand buses. From them, if bus

### Source page 66

transport were taken over and nationalised, what would be wrong? ‘If I alone have the authority, I will nationalise every branch of transport’—do not laugh thinking that I said it; I did not. Who said, ‘If I alone had the authority, I would nationalise every branch of transport’? The person who spoke at the economics meeting at Annamalai University on 26 February was Sir C. P. Ramaswami Aiyar. Why should you not accept this proposal? When you accept only his proposal that the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam should be banned, why should you not accept this proposal too? I ask: why should the bus industry not be nationalised?

In particular, I would like to add a news item I saw yesterday. There has been a report that the Government has given permission for 50 hire cars in Madras city to be placed with a private capitalist establishment. It has been reported that permission has been given to the Keevaraj Motor establishment for 50 hire cars. Those who now own taxis and let them out for hire in Madras city are, for the most part, people in small and middle-level circumstances. They are the ones who own these hire cars. I am not objecting to nationalising all these taxis outright. Until that happens, I ask whether these batches of 50 or 100 hire cars could not be kept from being handed to private establishments such as Keevaraj in a manner that harms the small and middle-level hire-car owners. What I hear about the condition on which they are going to grant this is that, if the Madras taxi drivers begin a strike,

### Source page 67

all the hire cars of this establishment must cooperate with us at that time. In short, I am deeply distressed to hear that this permission has been granted on the condition that they perform blackleg work. We must consider whether this arrangement could be used to defeat, break and crush even the struggles conducted by drivers for their legitimate demands.

Next, although it is accepted that agricultural growth and the advancement of agricultural industry are indispensable to the welfare of a country, and although all parties and sections in the country accept it, I cannot refrain from pointing out the differences and contradictions in the way it is being put into practice. We must compare ourselves with the pace of development that agriculture has attained in Japan, a country with about 60 lakh farmers, since the Second World War. When Mr. Mathiazhagan, while speaking, compared America and Russia, a question was raised as to whether we might compare ourselves with another country. When examining the economic condition of a country, comparing our country with other countries of the world can be a sound guide for the growth of our plans. On that basis, we must observe the development that has taken place in other countries. I am not saying that after seeing it we should lament or grieve that we are behind. What I am saying is that we should compare our country with other countries so that, seeing that development, we may prepare ourselves to catch up with it. Although the Government is said to be making efforts in many ways to increase agriculture,

### Source page 68

I cannot refrain from pointing out that, even if the objective is good, implementation is proceeding slowly and badly. Fertiliser needed for agriculture, as a Congress member pointed out, is neither available at a fair price nor does it arrive in time. Mr. Dhandayuthapani Pillai of the ruling party told this House the day before yesterday that it would work better if selected seed were distributed by individual farmers rather than by the Government. I am not saying this because I want the Government not to distribute it. I say it because the method of distribution must be orderly. The village assistance groups do not have sufficient training in multiplying and preparing selected seed. That is why I raise this point. Farmers say that in many places, using selected seed results in more weeds than they normally encounter when cultivating the field. I ask the members of this House and the Cabinet not to dismiss this as an argument made merely to condemn the Budget, but to recognise the actual situation and take appropriate action. We must not forget that this danger arises because the village assistance groups lack practical training, and that farmers are consequently put to difficulty. It is not enough to think that selected seed can be prepared scientifically; experience is also necessary. If a woman who has studied for a B.A. or M.A. but does not know how to cook is sent into the kitchen and told to cook, she may think that she must cook scientifically and hygienically and wash the brinjals and ladies' fingers with soap

### Source page 69

before making a curry. Just as one may imagine what that curry would be like, farmers are unable to obtain a good yield by sowing selected seed prepared by village assistance groups that lack experience.

Then there is the orchard scheme. They say that these orchards should be developed properly. When I travel to North Arcot, I often have to stop the car near the Katpadi railway gate because the gate is frequently closed. Beside it hangs a beautiful, attractive board saying “Orchard”. If one gets out of the car and looks for the orchard, it cannot be found. That orchard—the phantom orchard—is near the Katpadi railway gate. There is only a board there. There are only five or six pits. One can see neither fruit nor an orchard nor even plants. I do not condemn the scheme. I do not condemn the objective. I wish only to point out the contradictions and mistakes in the way it is implemented. As much as Rs.6,000 is spent through the Panchayat Union under the name of a bee-farm. But there must be supervision to ensure that this money is spent properly. I repeat again and again that I do not criticise the objective. I point out only the mistakes that arise in implementation. There must be proper supervision.

Research has been under way in Tamil Nadu, in this land of India, since 1950 into whether good agricultural results and higher yields can be achieved through music. But when the Agriculture Minister was asked about this yesterday, he said, “I do not know about it; I have not seen it.”

### Source page 70

I am surprised at that. Not because someone who has not even seen this is the Agriculture Minister, but because the Hon. Agriculture Minister does not even know of it; I am genuinely surprised. I brought this material with me so that I could give the supporting evidence to the Hon. Minister.

At Annamalai University there is a North Indian named D. C. S. Singh—this is proof that we bear no hatred toward individuals—who is the head of the plant-research department. I think it appropriate to set out here some details of the research he discovered. Experiments have been conducted at Annamalai University since December 1950. By playing the Charukesi raga on the nadaswaram, paddy production has increased by between 25 and 60 per cent. By the same method, groundnut production has increased by 45 per cent. At Ontario in Canada, playing European music on the violin increased wheat production by 66 per cent. The yield of tobacco seedlings increased by between 50 and 60 per cent. A loudspeaker was installed one foot above the field bund, and music was played for half an hour each day between 6 and 7 in the morning. The loudspeaker was generally pointed in the direction in which the wind was blowing. After soil tests, two plots of land of the same quality were selected; crops were grown with music played in one and without it in the other. The same fertilisers were applied in the same quantities on the same day to both. In the Pondicherry State, with the help of Government landowners, experiments were conducted on paddy at the following places: Keezhkadamangalam, Ramanathapuram, Irulankandhi,

### Source page 71

Karikalampakkam, Kavali Kuppam and Nainar Mandapam. The result was an increase in paddy production ranging from 8 to 57 per cent. The straw harvest rose by between 10 and 83 per cent. At Keezhkadamangalam, Kuthiraivaali Samba stimulated by music grew 5.48 inches taller than the crop without music. I have many such statistics with me. If the Hon. Finance Minister wishes, I am ready to give them to him. I do not understand whether the Hon. Agriculture Minister has no interest in this or has somehow failed to notice it. Not only in paddy: in sugarcane too, at the village of Uruvaiyaru, after the crop had grown for four and a half months, the Charukesi raga was played on the nadaswaram until harvest time. As a result, the yield was eight tons more per acre. Thus we have considerable evidence that yields can be increased through nadaswaram music.

An article about the researcher who conducted this work appeared in “Aften Posten”, an important newspaper in Northern Europe and Norway's largest daily. It said that there was a possibility that this Indian scientist, regarded as one who had rendered a service to humanity, might be considered deserving of the Nobel Prize. Since the article appeared in such an influential newspaper, it is thought that there is a possibility that the Nobel Committee may consider the matter. The Agriculture Department of the Pondicherry Government has been carrying on this research for the past two years. Further, in 1955 the Prithvi Trust gave researcher D. C. S. Singh the Prithvi Prize, namely Rs.2,000

and a gold medal, along with the title ‘Man of Philosophy’.

The same researcher wrote to Nehru asking that the Government of India take up this work. Nehru wrote back that there might be many difficulties in undertaking it and that the Agriculture Department should be approached for the effort. But our Agriculture Department has shown no interest whatsoever in this. How could it? Our Agriculture Minister did not even know that such research was taking place. I request that at least from now on interest be shown in it. (**Hon. Mr. K. Kamaraj:** The Minister has not concentrated on music; he has been concentrating only on agriculture.) I am only pointing out that music and agriculture have a harmonious connection.

Not only in agriculture; we cannot forget how far behind we are in industry as well. After the great clamour that arose in this country—or, if it will satisfy members of the ruling party, let me say after a great deal of shouting—one or two factories have come to this State. One or two projects have come to the State to that extent. The Salem steel plant has been under consideration since 1938. The Tuticorin deep-sea project and the Sethusamudram project—none of these projects finds a place here. Ships going to Tuticorin could avoid the unnecessary journey of more than five hundred miles around Ceylon. Such a project finds no place here. Most importantly, there is

a great deal of evidence of how far behind we are industrially. I will not set out all of it. I wish to give just one example. According to the figures for 1958–59, sugar production was 311 thousand tons in Bihar, 321 thousand tons in Bombay, 888 thousand tons in Uttar Pradesh and 74 thousand tons in Tamil Nadu. It may have risen a little by now. But what I particularly wish to point out is what Dr. P. Natarajan says on page 196 of the book *Indian Economics*: “South India and Bombay, unlike Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, are suited to sugarcane cultivation. Yet many mills have become established there.” I think the Hon. Finance Minister will be aware of this.

When a single industry flourishes in one place in this way, any slump in that industry will cause suffering to the people there. Industrial concentration in one place may lead to the neglect of other States. Moreover, when industries are concentrated in one place, if those regions

are destroyed in wartime, the whole country will suffer,” Dr. P. Natarajan says on page 174 of *Indian Economics*. If only in view of this importance, we must recognise the danger of all these industrial establishments being concentrated in one place, and our Ministers must fight with the Government of India to secure good, large projects for our Tamil Nadu, which stands foremost among the backward regions.

Already, a sum of four crore rupees that could have come from the Central Government has been cut. Such cut amounts must be demanded and secured boldly. In West Bengal, Shankar Das Banerjee has spoken out condemning the Central Government and has raised a battle cry asking whether it is just to withhold several fair shares of tax revenue. When the late Mr. P. C. Roy put forward the demands of his State, he raised the cry: “We frame our own plan; what authority has the Planning Commission over it? We ourselves will decide what has to be done in our State.” Our Ministers must acquire that kind of courage. What are our Ministers like? They are like a wrestler who knocks down ten or twenty wrestlers outside, then comes home and surrenders crying, “Wife, mother, great mother!” Our Ministers roar at me, “Just watch, we will finish you off.” But when they go to the Central Government, to the Delhi Government, they behave like that wrestler who fights all his bouts outside and then behaves quite differently when he gets home. This condition among our Ministers must change, and toward Delhi

they must boldly press and secure our demands.

Finally, I wish to mention one thing. The condition of the goldsmiths, who today are undergoing indescribable suffering, must be examined, and the Ministers seated in this august House must make the necessary efforts to find work for these people whose livelihood has been hit. Today the goldsmiths are floating in the river of unemployment. They do not know how to swim; they have no practice in swimming. I wish to point out that it is the responsibility of this Government to save them. I have been continuing to read the life story of the Hon. Kamaraj. When he was young, it is said, he told a friend of his, “Swim, swim,” in order to teach him to swim. When the friend refused, he caught hold of him and pushed him into the nearby river. Later, when that friend, unable to swim, struggled and thrashed about for his life, the Hon. Kamaraj brought him to the bank.

I greatly enjoyed reading of that incident in which he brought his friend to shore and saved him. In the same way, today you have caught these goldsmiths, who know no other work, and pushed them into the middle of the river. Just as on that day he pushed his friend into the river and then brought him to shore, I ask that you come forward to save these people too by providing them with employment. I conclude my speech by stating that I am unable to support the Budget brought forward on such an occasion, and by expressing my regret that we have not been fortunate enough to be able to welcome it. Thank you.
