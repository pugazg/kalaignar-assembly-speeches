Meanwhile, if we take the new industrial complexes being created through SIPCOT — Thiru Ranganathan asked what the new ones were. To explain that: the announcements for 1996–97 were the Cheyyar industrial complex, the Irungattukottai industrial complex, and the second-phase Gummidipoondi industrial complex. As far as Cheyyar is concerned, the first phase of the Cheyyar industrial complex was planned to be implemented over 600 acres. For this, 260 acres of land have been acquired. A consultant has been appointed to prepare the master plan for the Cheyyar industrial complex. The Tamil Nadu Water Supply and Drainage Board has been asked to examine bringing water from the Palar riverbed to the Cheyyar industrial complex.

The Irungattukottai industrial complex — it was planned to establish the Irungattukottai industrial complex over 1,829 acres. For this, 1,314 acres have been acquired. So far, 700 acres have been allotted for industrial promoters. Of this, I wish to inform you that 532 acres have been allotted to the Hyundai motor-car factory alone. The infrastructure facilities developed by SIPCOT at Irungattukottai include roads developed for a length of 10 kilometres—

Next, regarding the announcements for 1997–98 and their implementation: in 1997–98 it was announced that industrial complexes would be created at Sriperumbudur, Nemili and Vadamangalam. To state their progress, the Sriperumbudur SIPCOT industrial complex is planned to be developed over 2,469 acres. 292 acres have been acquired. Of this, 125 acres have been given to the Saint-Gobain glass factory. A consultant has been appointed for development of the complex. Project estimates have been prepared. Tenders have been invited for creating infrastructure facilities such as roads. Preliminary studies concerning soil, water and so on have been undertaken. It is planned to establish the Nemili satellite town over 2,429 acres and the Vadamangalam industrial park over 1,408 acres — industrial complexes — and I also inform the House that land-acquisition work for them is under way.

Tamil Nadu’s exports, which stood at Rs. 6,781 crore in 1991–92, rose to Rs. 18,500 crore in 1996–97. I inform you that in 1997–98 this crossed Rs. 20,000 crore. I am happy to inform you that Tamil Nadu accounts for 17 per cent of India’s exports.

In the postgraduate business-administration study report published by ‘P.T. Kelam’ in December 1997, it is stated that Tamil Nadu ranked third in attracting investors most strongly. Our Krishnasamy too spoke here about the advantages and disadvantages that may arise from foreign direct investment. He expressed views such as that we should not become excessively vulnerable to them. He spoke about the nuclear-bomb test. He also spoke about the extent to which foreign countries are warning India today. But after the Kazhagam Government assumed office, from May 1996 to January 1998, the total amount of foreign direct investment proposed and under implementation was Rs. 43,515 crore. If from this we deduct the amounts relating to investments whose accounts were closed during the period and investments that were abandoned — if Rs. 8,522 crore is deducted — then up to January 1998 it is Rs. 34,993 crore. That is approved investment. Approval has been obtained for it. Within this Rs. 34,993 crore there are many possibilities for industries to invest here and come here. If we look at what those industries are: basic chemicals, Rs. 16,329 crore; petroleum products—

If we consider the social effects that may arise from foreign direct investment, it cannot be denied that, as a result of the liberalisation of the Indian economy, foreign direct investment in India is on the rise. No one can deny that foreign direct investment helps bring modern technology from various parts of the world into the State. Even though in some sections of political circles today there is talk to the extent of saying that even that technology is not needed, we cannot forget how necessary that technology is in today’s scientific world, in a developing scientific world, and particularly how necessary it is for a country such as India, containing large numbers of people below the poverty line.

In addition to the State’s own financial resources, substantial foreign financial resources can be brought in through this foreign capital. Companies that operate successfully with superior foreign commercial and managerial expertise are able to expand their industries in Tamil Nadu. The professional skills and professional knowledge of our engineers, junior supervisors, employees and others at various levels increase considerably. Their value and employment opportunities increase. Along with an increase in overall productive capacity in the State economy, a path is also created for generating numerous new employment opportunities.
