## Kalaignar's Legislative Assembly Speeches on the Financial Statement

**Speech: 5**  
**Date: 2.7.1962**

**Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi:** Honourable Speaker! The Honourable Finance Minister has placed before this House the financial statement for 1962-63, and it has now been taken up for discussion. At this time I wish to state my views on it. As usual, he has issued this statement after pouring out statistics.

I wish to point out that the statistics now given are, as usual, mutually contradictory. Our new Finance Minister has stated that, despite chemical fertiliser not being available in sufficient quantity during the Second Five-Year Plan period, it was possible to produce an additional 9 lakh tons of foodgrains. But something different has been said earlier in this very House. When the former Finance Minister presented the 1961-62 Budget, he stated that during the Second Five-Year Plan period approximately seven or eight lakh tons of additional food were produced. I do not understand how, in the meantime, another one or two lakh tons entered the financial statement. I wish to point out that this statement has been compiled with such contradictory statistics, and with an abundance of statistics designed to attract the people.

### Source page 50

It is also said that by the end of the Third Five-Year Plan, foodgrain production must be increased by 17 lakh tons. Looking at the old target, it had been said that during the Second and Third Five-Year Plan periods an additional 12.79 lakh tons of food would be produced. That much ought to have been produced. But that additional 12.79 lakh tons of food has not been produced. Food production has increased only to the extent shown by the two contradictory figures I cited earlier. When targets are being fulfilled to this extent, I do not understand on what basis we can confidently believe the target given by the Finance Minister that food production will increase by 17 lakh tons.

Our former Finance Minister gave as the reason for our failure to reach the target during the Second Plan period that fertiliser was not available in sufficient quantity. I wish to know what assurances are now being given that more fertiliser will be available. In 1956-57, sulphate fertiliser was supplied to the extent of 1.2 lakh tons. If, after four years, when 1960-61 is coming to an end, only 1.3 lakh tons could be supplied, how much of an increase is that? We are told about fertiliser produced at Neyveli and fertiliser produced in other ways. In the previous Budget it was stated that by the end of the Third Plan period, 4 lakh tons of ammonium sulphate fertiliser would be required for irrigated lands. Looking at these figures, we must consider whether the efforts relating to fertiliser are sufficient to increase food production by 17 lakh tons.

Next, seed farms are essential for food production. The 1958-59 Budget statement said that by 1959-60, 360 seed farms—

### Source page 51

—were to be established. Having planned for 360 seed farms, it was then shown that only 130 farms could be established, and instead of increasing their number, it was strongly stated in 1959-60 that food production would be adequately served if the farms already begun were run efficiently. They have said that 136 seed farms have been established. It has also been estimated that we need 210 seed farms. Through today's financial statement, have they rejected the earlier decision? Or what efforts are they going to make to reach the target of 210 seed farms? Nothing is stated about that in this report.

With no good progress in fertiliser, nothing shown that inspires confidence, a plan for 360 farms but only 136 established, satisfaction expressed as though that were enough, and an acknowledgment that 210 farms would enable production to be increased properly without stating any way to establish those 210 farms, I wish once again to emphasise my doubt as to how they are going to reach the target and increase food production by 17 lakh tons.

As for industry, our Finance Minister has stated in this year's financial statement that our State has not fallen behind in industrial development. Today the Finance Minister who runs the Government has revealed to us an astonishing novelty unknown to all of us. As though, wherever one looked in Tamil Nadu, if one stumbled one would fall into a factory and if one tripped one would fall into a factory—

### Source page 52

—he has revealed to us the great truth that our State is not lagging behind in industry, as if asking whether none of us had seen it.

As regards industrial development, both the Second Five-Year Plan draft and the Third Five-Year Plan draft stated that our State was industrially backward. When the Government in the North rejected the Rs. 399 crore that was requested and only Rs. 290 crore was given, I cannot understand the Minister's sudden claim that Tamil Nadu has advanced industrially, or that our State has not lagged behind other States. I ask the Finance Minister who has presented this financial statement whether the people are being subjected to deception in this manner.

Again and again the Neyveli scheme is cited in this House like a worn gramophone record: “Neyveli scheme, Neyveli scheme!” As for other industries, “Poovanga” is under consideration. Research is going on. The site has been inspected; the land has been purchased. When matters remain at these stages, I do not know what industrial development the Finance Minister can show to say that industry has grown in this State, or that we can compete with other States.

To whom have most factories been entrusted? Again and again they have been entrusted to the private sector, and continue to be entrusted to it. During the Second Plan period, 908 companies were registered to carry on industry in our State. Of these, 66 were public limited and 842 private limited. Next, it is stated that we have advanced greatly in chemical production. Who is this “we”? We—

### Source page 53

—I thought that when they said “we,” they meant the Government. But when we look at whom they mean, it is establishments such as Dharangadhara and India Cements. The Minister describes the progress of the private sector as “we have progressed.” Because this “we” represents Dharangadhara, Viscose, Seshashayee, Madras Aluminium Company and Parry Company, poor people are boarding ships and undergoing many hardships, saying, “We cannot find well-being under this Government. We cannot live if we remain here; therefore, let us board ships.” I wish to place before this House as an accusation that the representatives of that word “we” have not framed industrial plans in a way that enables the poor to live.

Why should the private sector be developed? What are the reasons for it? I expect this Cabinet to explain why this Government is increasingly developing an economic plan that mixes the public and private sectors together like a kadambam. I wish to point out that if the Government prevents the plundering profits made by the private sector and takes those industries over to run them itself, there will be no need to torment the people by imposing more and more taxes.

This year taxes amounting to Rs. 7 crore have been imposed. Before such taxes of Rs. 7 crore were imposed, and almost even before this matter came up in this House, the Chief Minister had been telling the general public, “Taxes are coming, taxes are coming,” and had prepared them to some extent...

**Hon. Mr. M. Bhaktavatsalam:** The Honourable Chief Minister did not say seven crores. When several Honourable Members said in this House that there should be no tax, the Chief Minister said, “What is wrong if a tax is imposed?”

**Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi:** Even if the Honourable Chief Minister did not say seven crores, during the election the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam understood that taxes amounting to Rs. 7 crore would be imposed and warned the people through posters it issued. But because the people did not heed that warning, the result is that you have come here as 139 members and we as 50 members. I hope that at least from now on the voters of this country will realise that those who say one thing until the election will later impose taxes as thanks for the votes cast for them.

Today taxes amounting to seven crore rupees have been imposed. The Finance Minister has stated that, when development schemes are kept in mind, attempts to impose taxes cannot be avoided. Everyone knows that the interim Budget for 1962-63 was presented in this House. What did the former Finance Minister state in it? He said that revenue was Rs. 92.71 crore, expenditure Rs. 99.67 crore, and the deficit Rs. 7 crore. He also indicated that a revised estimate might come later. Having said in the interim Budget that there was a deficit of seven crore rupees, because that Budget was presented here before the election, why was no way stated then to make up this seven crore rupees, while different reasons are now being given? I ask the Minister himself to examine this in the House and tell us.

What did the former Finance Minister say at that time about how the seven crore rupees would be made good?

“...In general, the revenue from the Central Government's tax heads has been growing. In particular, the revenue obtained through taxes imposed on commodities—

### Source page 55

—is rising considerably. Therefore, there is scope for the share that our State can receive from these tax heads to increase substantially. This gives us some measure of confidence. Further, the Third Finance Commission is now examining the share to be given to the States from Central revenues. We have explained to the Finance Commission that most State taxes are not sufficiently elastic, and that State Governments have to assume more and more responsibility for meeting the basic needs of the people. It is expected that the Finance Commission's report, which is to be submitted shortly, will provide funds to the States in an appropriate manner....” This was said when the interim Budget was presented in this House.

Members of the ruling party who spoke in support of that Budget praised it by saying, “What a Government this is—there is a deficit of seven crore rupees and yet it imposes no tax!” Where have all those reasons gone now? If they had then said, “Even if there is a deficit of seven crore rupees, we will not be afraid; we will impose taxes,” perhaps people might not have voted for them. What meaning can there be other than that they said nothing then, won the election, became Ministers and members of the ruling party, and are now imposing taxes? What happened to the promise given then by the Finance Minister? Did the Finance Commission fail to render justice? I believe the Honourable Minister will answer this in the House.

When presenting the Budget for 1961-62, the former Finance Minister stated in his speech, “In a country functioning on a democratic basis, there is a limit to raising funds through taxation”—

### Source page 56

—When I say “former Finance Minister,” I assume the new Finance Minister will not enter into the argument that “that was different, this is different.” These Ministers often say that if plans are to be implemented, there must be a stable Government. Therefore, even though I refer to the former Finance Minister and the new Finance Minister, I do so on the basis of their own principle that there must be a stable Government: since both belong to the Government of the same ruling party, the same policy should be reflected.

Where has the sentence “In a country functioning on a democratic basis, there is a limit to raising funds through taxation” gone in this financial statement? What did the Finance Minister of Mysore State say two days ago? “During the Third Five-Year Plan period we are expected to impose taxes to the extent of Rs. 42 crore; it is not possible to do so. During the Third Five-Year Plan period we can impose taxes only to the extent of Rs. 20 crore; the Planning Commission must provide the shortfall.” The Mysore Government has made that request to the Planning Commission in the North. Why should our Finance Minister not say the same thing boldly? Does our Finance Minister lack courage? Was he not the Police Minister last time? I therefore ask him to summon his courage and certainly fight with the Planning Commission.

With regard to the taxes now being imposed to make good a deficit of more than nine crore rupees, it may superficially be said today that they are taxes imposed on somebody else. But by these taxes—

### Source page 57

—I wish to say here that it is largely the general public who are affected.

The tax on buses has been increased. It has been increased at the rates of five rupees, ten rupees and fifteen rupees per quarter. The Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam does not oppose this tax imposed on buses. But we must examine on whose heads this tax is being placed. Well-known bus owners have already issued statements in the newspapers saying clearly that this will be placed on the heads of passengers through bus fares. They have issued statements saying, “We are going to raise bus fares.” Although the maximum bus fare is 7½ paise, today fares are charged only at about four to six paise. After this tax comes into force, if they use the law available to them and begin charging fares up to seven or seven-and-a-half paise, I wish to make it clear here that those affected by this tax will largely be the poor and working people.

Therefore, while imposing this tax, the Minister must give an assurance here that “the Government will provide adequate protection to ensure that fares definitely do not rise.” The Government must amend the law so that the fare is only five or six paise. Only if such an assurance is given and this tax is imposed will the public be protected. Otherwise, it will amount to saying to the bus owners, “I will pretend to beat you; you pretend to cry.” The demand that the bus industry should be nationalised—

### Source page 58

—has been ignored, and imposing a tax on buses today is like letting go of the thumbai and grabbing the tail. If the bus industry is nationalised, I point out to this Government that it will certainly yield more revenue than this tax.

As for lorries, small lorry operators do not earn profits on the scale of bus owners. The Government should think about taxing them. Those who own ten or twenty lorries may not be affected by this tax, but those who make a living with one or two lorries will certainly be affected. The Cabinet must think deeply about this. Taxing lorries will affect poor people and will cause the price of foodstuffs to rise. The Government must consider these matters.

As for imposing a tax on electricity consumption, “low tension” electricity is used for agriculture. It needs exemption from the tax. In the case of houses, if there are luxurious lights, coloured lights, banana-stem lights and the like, electricity tax may be imposed on them. But I request that provision be made to grant exemption to ordinary middle-class households that have only a 15-watt or 25-watt light glowing like a firefly.

I wish to speak about land tax. During the past thirty years the quality of lands has changed in many ways. Rain-fed land has become fertile irrigated land; fertile land has become land that looks to the sky for rain—

### Source page 59

—Taking into account the changes and new conditions that have arisen over thirty years, a committee should be appointed to classify the quality of lands properly. Such a committee may make changes in the existing land-tax rate. The newly imposed land tax should now be withdrawn. After that committee is appointed, changes in land tax can be considered.

It is said that by doubling the horse-racing tax rates, Rs. 24 lakh will be obtained. People have been saying that the system of horse racing should be abolished. Because the prohibition scheme was introduced, liquor was abolished. This Government, which has earned the distinction of having removed that evil practice from the country while losing the Rs. 17 crore that had been received through the liquor tax, should be prepared to abolish horse racing too and lose the small revenue obtained from it.

Finally, it is deeply regrettable that in this year's financial statement the Minister has said nothing about the problem of rising prices that is eating away at the lives of the people. The former Finance Minister has said that it is because of rising prices that the economic sector has been affected, economic growth has declined, and the progress of our plans has been affected. I ask whether the new Finance Minister will pay attention to that, and with this I conclude my remarks in this debate.
