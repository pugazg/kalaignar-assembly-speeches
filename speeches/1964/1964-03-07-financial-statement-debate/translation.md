## Kalaignar's Legislative Assembly Speeches on the Financial Statement

**Speech: 7**  
**Date: 7.3.1964**

**Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi:** Mr. Speaker, the Opposition members who have taken part in the debate on the 1964–65 Financial Statement and spoken here have placed before this House a variety of views, as well as several problems that have neither been pointed out nor given relief in this Budget. In particular, they have spoken of the neglect of the N.G.O. issue, in which a demand has been made for an increase in dearness allowance; of the disparity between the pay of Central Government and State Government employees; and, though page after page of this Budget discusses the education problem, they have expressed regret that not even one line points to suitable measures for improving the condition of teachers. Opposition members have presented their views on these matters here.

The question of building houses for teachers has been raised in this House many times. For a long time, the demand has been placed before this House that houses should be built for teachers in villages just as they are built for policemen. Perhaps thinking that legislators would be pleased, this Budget contains the news that Rs. 40 lakhs has been allotted for legislators' lodging—

### Source page 77

—facilities. Whether a legislators' hostel costing Rs. 40 lakhs has to be built with such urgency and necessity is itself a question for debate.

**Hon. Mr. M. Bhaktavatsalam:** If you state your view on that categorically, we are prepared to reconsider it on that basis.

**Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi:** Perhaps the Minister thinks that I will categorically declare that legislators do not need lodging facilities and that he can thereby create some hostility towards me among legislators; in his characteristically skilful manner, he seems to be trying to corner me. *(Laughter.)* Such a large sum as Rs. 40 lakhs is not necessary for this. It is my humble view that the amount can be reduced substantially.

For in one place in this Budget, whether at the hospital attached to the medical college at Thanjavur, which has 400 beds, or at many other hospitals, when we look at facilities for patients, we cannot fail to see the moving sight of hundreds of patients who have no beds to lie on and are left on the floors of the verandahs. A major portion of this money can be spent to increase 400 beds to 600 in such hospitals, and not only at Thanjavur but also to provide facilities for patients in many hospitals where people are suffering. If Rs. 5 lakhs is being spent to expand by four hundred beds, another Rs. 10 lakhs could be spent to provide patients with twice as many facilities.

### Source page 78

It would be enough to improve the present facilities for legislators to some extent. I wish to state that I do not consider it appropriate to spend Rs. 40 lakhs on that.

On the rise in prices, the Minister has, as usual, supplied a kind of research essay in this Budget as well, but has not stated what suitable methods, immediate measures or firm actions will be taken to control prices. Still, the position has moved from asking, “Where have prices risen? Where have prices risen?” to the Minister recognising that prices have risen and accepting that they must be brought down; that much, perhaps, may deserve appreciation.

It is not enough merely for the prices of food articles to fall. We in the Opposition have said that the prices of the things needed by the farmers who produce them must also be controlled. It is on the basis of that idea that the Minister has stated one thing in the Budget: agricultural production costs have also increased; in keeping with these, we must formulate a policy for a fair, stable minimum-price scheme for agricultural produce.

I wish to draw attention to the fact that such a policy was enacted in England in 1947 and defined within its agricultural programme. Under that law the Government was given authority to ensure the production of food and stable prices. Secondly, agricultural conditions were reviewed every year and the prices of agricultural products were fixed accordingly. The ruling party has been proclaiming in the country that if production increases, the rise in prices will come down—

### Source page 79

—but if we ask whether that policy of increasing production has been implemented properly, the answer is no. I wish to mention some provisions found in England's 1947 law which were intended to give farmers greater concern for increasing production and a sound desire to do so.

If the owner or cultivator of a piece of land failed to maintain it properly or failed to cultivate it properly, authority was given to bring it under Government supervision. If the proper level of production was not achieved in that year, the Government had authority either to purchase the land from its owner or to remove the cultivator. The administration of England's Agriculture Department says that this law laid down ways and means for agriculture to be carried on properly.

Farmers must be encouraged. In particular, all of us know well the damage caused by rain and floods in Thanjavur and in certain areas of Tiruchirappalli last year and this year. Yet the substantial farming population in the Thanjavur and Tiruchirappalli areas is now under pressure to pay this instalment together with the instalment due last time. They have been put in the hardship of paying 45 paise from last time and 45 paise this time, making 90 paise together. The matter has been brought to the Minister's attention, but it has not been attended to. Even if it has not been attended to now, I request that the Council of Ministers undertake efforts to encourage and hearten them, at least by granting tax relief next year. This—

### Source page 80

—Budget, the Minister has stated in two or three places in the Financial Statement, is based on the policy of democratic socialism adopted at the Bhubaneswar Conference. I consider it necessary only to recall here a statement made by Mahatma Gandhi on socialism:

“Socialism begins with the first person who transforms himself. If there is such a one, zeros may be added beside that one. One becomes ten, ten becomes a hundred, and every zero thus added multiplies what precedes it tenfold. But if the person who begins is himself a zero, however many zeros are added thereafter, their value remains zero. The paper and time spent writing those zeros are wasted.”

Gandhi said that if the beginning itself is zero, the zeros subsequently added have no value. The Congress rulers who speak of socialism, democratic socialism and Bhubaneswar socialism—do they at least conduct themselves according to the socialist policies within the Congress party? Has the Congress party truly given value to socialist policy? We must see whether they repeatedly use the sweet word “socialism” among the people as a device for harvesting votes at elections.

If there were genuine faith in socialism, the Congress party today would not have to depend on so many capitalists and mill owners; if not for many capitalists and wealthy men, the Congress party would have no prospect of electoral victory—

### Source page 81

—and so, on one side, they foster many capitalists and mill owners, create greater profits for them, fatten them, strengthen the party through them, and today speak of socialism. In Gandhi's terms, I wish to point out that this socialism did not begin with a “one”; it is socialism begun from zero and nothing else.

It is precisely against such a contradictory policy that the people of Tamil Nadu have recently been giving their considered verdict. Such a verdict was given at Tiruchengode. It continued at Tiruvannamalai. Now it has been given in the Madras Corporation election. The Congress friends who suffered defeat have not merely failed to accept that verdict; they have gone so far as to mock it. We know very well that questions were raised in the Legislature along the lines of: “It was not a victory for policy; it was not a victory for the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam; it was a victory obtained because of some cinema actors. Should cinema actors campaign in elections?”

Those who think that cinema actors taking part in the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam's election campaign causes great irritation in their minds—have they forgotten that cinema actors worked for Kennedy's victory in the American presidential election? When Krishna Menon contested an election in Bombay in the Indian subcontinent, why have they forgotten that prominent cinema actors from the northern part worked for Krishna Menon's victory? I do not understand why, even if a few Ministers in the Congress party do not hold it, so many ruling-party members in the Congress party seem to have the notion that cinema is somehow untouchable and that cinema is an art that should not exist in the country—

### Source page 82

—at all.

That same Congress party received Rs. 10,000 for the Tamil Nadu Congress Fund from the Film Chamber of Commerce—four days before the election, on the 18th or 19th, I think—from the Madras film-trade association. Yet when they themselves now raise questions about whether cinema actors may take part in election campaigning, I can only laugh. The Tamil Nadu Congress Committee received Rs. 10,000 as election funds from the Film Chamber and now looks at the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam and calls it a cinema party; that is the great irony.

A lady who once shone like a lamp of fame in the world of art and whose fame remains undimmed even today says in writing her life story:

“In those days, if I did not sing on a Congress platform, no crowd would gather! Mr. Sathyamurthi would seek me out wherever I was and bring me there. The meeting would begin only after I sang the national song. After my husband died I stopped taking part in meetings. Later, when Mahatma Gandhi came to Tamil Nadu, he called me and ordered me: ‘Having lost your husband, are you going to spend your whole life thinking of him and grieving? You must take part in meetings again.’”

It was Kodumudi Kokilam Sundarambal, who was a light in the world of art, who wrote this in her life story. It is amusing to speak as though the Congress party has set cinema and cinema people aside, as though it wants no connection with them—

### Source page 83

—and as though only the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam lives in association with cinema actors.

Those who embraced defeat in the Corporation election say not only that we associated ourselves with cinema actors, but also: “You published Chinnasami's picture; that is why you won.” They ask whether publishing Chinnasami's picture was proper. Our friend Anbil Dharmalingam too asked: is it wrong even to pay respect to a person who has died? The Chief Minister immediately rose and said, “You may pay respect, but publishing the picture and canvassing votes with it is not proper.”

Publishing Chinnasami's picture is said to be improper. But was it proper to publish the picture of Tirupati Venkatachalapathi alongside the bull symbol? I ask the Chief Minister this with great respect. Placing the bull beside Tirupati Venkatachalapathi as an election image amounts to seeking votes and victory by using God through religious propaganda; in my view, that is wrong under election law. Congress candidates distributed pictures of Tirupati Venkatachalapathi together with the election symbol not in one ward but in many wards. If they may put out the picture of Periyasami, should they become angry when we ask whether it is wrong for us to put out this Chinnasami picture?

One lady, while speaking, said that after the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam won, they bought a bull for 17 rupees, drove it about and beat it. The Hon. Minister rose in great haste and asked, “Did they eat the meat?”

### Source page 84

I too had a doubt: seeing the haste with which the Minister asked, it sounded as though he were asking, “Is there any meat left?” I saw this report in a newspaper under the heading “Letters to the Editor”; it said that a bull was bought for 17 rupees somewhere around Royapettah and chased. The lady said it was in Nungambakkam. How amusing it is to suggest that a bull could be bought in Madras for seventeen rupees!

What I ask them is this: if cattle are available that cheaply, even I am prepared to buy ten or fifteen and raise them at home. But when it was said that a bull had been bought for 17 rupees and killed, a doubt occurred to me. Periyar Ramasami, who has recently been strongly supporting the Congress, said that one should eat beef in order to be healthy. I wondered whether the Congressmen and the Dravidar Kazhagam friends might have beaten the bull in order to carry out Periyar's command.

There is no evidence whatsoever that members of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam engaged in such a cruel act. I therefore regret that a responsible lady in the Legislature should speak in that manner about something for which there is no evidence.

Today I will not find fault with the responsible Ministers in the Congress. But knowingly or unknowingly, a frightening force is entering the Congress party itself. That force is spreading in alliance with the Congress. Recently I read in a newspaper what the Hon. Minister Kakkan said at Ramanathapuram. What he clearly—

### Source page 85

—stated there was this:

“As a result of China's aggression, the true form of the Indian Communists and the whole course of their conduct have become well known to us. In such a situation, supporting them is a great sin; not merely a sin, but a grave offence knowingly committed, and a disgrace.”

This is what the respected Police Minister stated at a meeting in Ramanathapuram on February 11. Yet the allies who have joined together, calling themselves progressive forces and saying that all three will remain united and defeat the reactionary forces, engaged—through this drama behind the curtain—in election propaganda that subjected the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam to murderous hostility. Even after the election they continue the same propaganda.

A life prisoner is generally sentenced to twenty years, and if he is released earlier, the police keep him under watch for the remainder of that period. But perhaps a life prisoner who enters politics is not subject to such surveillance under this Government. Since the Congress Government supports such a life prisoner, we do not know whether that prisoner is free of such surveillance. If such a situation is allowed to grow, I submit with great humility, in the interests of political civility, that in the end only life prisoners will remain in Congress politics and no one else will remain.

That life prisoner said at a meeting, “Even if I have to mount the gallows, I shall destroy the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam.” The Congress party's principal newspaper published that statement in bold type. The Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam is an ordinary Opposition party. If destroying it would require him to mount the gallows, what else can the underlying—

### Source page 86

—intention be except to eliminate certain particular leaders of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam? What protection do we in the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam have? He speaks in a manner that says, “Do you know whom we have joined? The ruling party gives us shelter. Therefore nothing can be done to us.”

Another friend writes in a newspaper. My tongue shrinks from saying it. I ask the respected Ministers seated here. Referring to Mr. Rajagopalachari, whose portrait hangs in this great House and who is worthy of our respect, a friend from the Tamil National Party wrote in a newspaper: “Achariar! Kamaraj's slipper is worth more than your head.”

Through you, I say to those in the Cabinet: you have taken such people into your alliance. If the portrait of a learned man such as Achariar need not adorn this great House, and if something else is said to be of greater worth, let us consider hanging that instead. Is Achariar's portrait fit to be here? Is he somehow fixing a price on Achariar's head? If you continue to foster all this, and they speak by joining the Congress party to themselves and saying that this Cabinet supports them, what will become of Tamil Nadu's future? How dark will Tamil Nadu's future become? We must think about the extent to which political civility will deteriorate.

Another legislator—a respected friend, a man worthy of regard—while speaking at Nagapattinam—

### Source page 87

—referred to “those good-for-nothing fellows Annadurai and Karunanidhi.” There was immediately a great disturbance. The next day, the Congress candidate who had organised the meeting met the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam friends and expressed regret over the incident. He expressed regret for that speech, saying, “None of you should feel hurt by such words.”

Political civility has reached this extreme in the election. Where will this end?

Yesterday, the Hon. Member Mr. Rajaram told the story of Bhasmasura for some reason. Whether or not the Bhasmasura story fits anyone else, it fits the Congress leaders of today very well. They are the ones who have allowed such a situation to grow.

Because it was allowed to grow, at a Congress party meeting held on 27.2.1964 at Madhava Perumal Koil Street in this very city of Madras, someone said:

“The Ministers of this country, especially the Chief Minister, appear to be favourable to the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam. If he is favourable in that way, the first blow we deliver—I use the word ‘blow’ brutally—the first blow must fall on the back of Chief Minister Bhaktavatsalam.”

The reason I mention this is to remind you that if the Aladdin's genie you have nurtured is not given work, it will devour the very person who commanded it. Likewise, like Bhasmasura placing his hand on the head of Lord Siva who granted him the boon—in the story our friend recalled—they have now come to place their hand on the Minister's own head. If this is the fate of Ministers themselves, what is to be our fate?

### Source page 88

Stenographers come to Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam meetings and record what those in the Opposition say. But they are not sent to meetings held on behalf of the Congress party. I wish to state that if the Ministers would at least hereafter graciously send them to take notes of such speeches, it would help them understand the situation properly. *(Bell rung.)*

I referred to the story of Bhasmasura. There is another Bakasura-like force called Hindi which is disfiguring Tamil Nadu. Although the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam has openly assumed the posture of a non-violent struggle to destroy and eliminate that Bakasura-like force, poets, scholars and good people throughout Tamil Nadu have all pointed out that if Hindi domination spreads in Tamil Nadu, grave danger will befall Tamil Nadu and the people of Tamil Nadu will become second-class citizens.

Today our Chief Minister has been invited to Delhi to discuss what language candidates taking the Union Public Service Commission examination for Central Government posts should use. At this time, as he prepares to go, I wish to place before the Chief Minister a request on behalf of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam. Minister Mr. Nanda has stated that regional languages such as Tamil are not suitable for writing examinations for Central Government posts. I wish to ask: I do not understand to what extent Hindi, which today has the status of one regional language, has risen above regional languages such as Tamil.

### Source page 89

At such a critical time our Chief Minister has been invited there. He is going. We earnestly and humbly request the Chief Minister who is going there—not only on behalf of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, but on behalf of the Opposition, on behalf of the people of Tamil Nadu and on behalf of the non-Hindi-speaking States—to go there, sit upright with courage, and fight for the people of Tamil Nadu.

He should not think that Congressmen will be upset if, in his mind, he speaks like the Bengal lion P. C. Roy. He must not forget that on this one issue the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam stands behind him. Not only members of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam; even within the Congress there are many who are afraid to express this view openly and who stand behind him.

Through the Hon. Speaker, I wish to say to the Hon. Minister that, before he goes there, I have just one request: convene this House and begin a debate in order to learn the views of its members; obtain those views, and let the Chief Minister go to the city of Delhi and, like Cheran Senguttuvan, like Nedunchezhiyan the Pandyan, like Karikala Peruvalathan, sit upright and go with the courage that he is serving Tamil Nadu and his own land.

I offer him my wishes: “Go and return; go and return victorious.” Even though I had been accused of conspiracy for having used these words when people were going to burn the Constitution, when I now say, “Go and return; go and return victorious,” I say it for the sake of protecting Tamil Nadu and protecting Tamil. With this I conclude my speech. Thank you.
