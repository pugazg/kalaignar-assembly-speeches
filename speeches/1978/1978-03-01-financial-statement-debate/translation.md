Speech: 16                                              Date: 1.3.1978

**Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi:** Mr Speaker, I wish to present my views, on behalf of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, on the financial statement for the year 1978-79.

Various members and party leaders who spoke here have pointed out that this House proceeded directly to the financial statement without the Governor's Address—the Government's policy document—being delivered, that this was not in keeping with the traditions of the House, and that it was deeply regrettable that such a lapse had occurred. They have also said that the members of this august House were thereby deprived of an opportunity to debate the Government's policy for six or seven days. I find myself in a position of endorsing those views.

When the debate arises as to whether there should be an office called Governor at all, the answer of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam is a different one. But so long as that office exists, abandoning this year the convention under which the Government's policy exposition is placed before the financial statement through the person holding that office is, in truth, not acceptable. I am duty-bound to point this out to the Leader of the House, my friend the Hon. Finance Minister.

In the 1978-79 financial statement, the Finance Minister has said that the revenue expected this year is Rs.680 crore and that this too is a deficit budget. Seven or eight months ago, when the financial statement was placed before this House, the size of the deficit was pointed out and certain methods were stated by which it would be made good; foremost among them, it was said, was that the deficit would be met by eliminating bribery, graft and corruption.

When we now see that the deficit has not been made good and has instead grown further, members of the House cannot avoid wondering whether the bribery, graft and corruption referred to have still not been eliminated. Members of the ruling party said, “Look, in this year's budget we have shown revenue of Rs.680 crore. The Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam Government showed revenue of only about Rs.500 crore in its financial statement. What great progress this is!” They even brought it under the heading of a major achievement and spoke here. I do not think all of them said this deliberately. Some may have spoken with an understanding of the matter; some may have spoken without understanding it.

But one thing I must explain to this House is this: when the 1968-69 financial statement was placed before this House, the amount expected as revenue was Rs.248 crore. In 1975-76, that is, at the time when the Kazhagam Government was dismissed—

—the expected revenue announced in the financial statement of this Government was Rs.501 crore. Then, under President's Rule in 1976-77, Rs.501 crore rose to Rs.576 crore; and when Finance Minister H.M. Patel presented the 1977-78 financial statement in Parliament, expected revenue was Rs.635 crore. We must not forget that it is that growth to Rs.635 crore which has now taken the form, in this 1978-79 financial statement, of expected revenue of Rs.680 crore.

If we take three particularly important departments—education, agriculture and health—and compare how much was spent on them in 1968-69 and how much is spent now, we must also consider how the figure has grown step by step. In 1968-69, when revenue was expected to be Rs.248 crore, the allocation for these three important departments was Rs.47.10 crore. Under Kazhagam rule, in 1975-76, when revenue was estimated at Rs.501 crore, Rs.181.78 crore was allotted to education, agriculture and health. Under President's Rule in 1976-77, when revenue was estimated at Rs.576 crore, a total of Rs.195.64 crore was allotted to these three departments. In the 1977-78 financial statement placed in Parliament by H.M. Patel, when revenue was estimated at Rs.635 crore, the allocation for education, agriculture and health was—

### Source page 359

—Rs.229.69 crore. Now, when revenue is expected to be Rs.680 crore, Rs.253.59 crore has been allotted to these three departments. Thus it has risen from Rs.229 crore to Rs.253 crore. Revenue too, which had grown to Rs.635 crore when Mr Patel presented the budget, has now reached Rs.680 crore.

Why do I mention this? Only to point out that, as revenues keep increasing from time to time, the corresponding allocations too keep increasing. That is why I have placed these details before this august House.

Thus, in every financial statement, whether there is an elected Government or President's Rule because those elected by the people are not in office, these statements indicate the same position: allocations rise in keeping with revenue.

In the conventional sense, as the Professor pointed out yesterday, continuing expenditure and allocations are made in accordance with revenue. To that extent, we welcome it. We recognise that as the position.

What we want to know afresh is: what are the policies and schemes of this new Government, and what are the works and achievements to be carried out through them? That is what we wish to ask in this House.

Without such policy announcements or new schemes, the amount has merely increased because allocations have been made to various departments in keeping with revenue that has been rising for many years—

—and to those who ask what happened during Kazhagam rule, I wish to say this: during the Kazhagam Government, standing by the policy laid down by Arignar Anna, many schemes were announced in every financial statement on a policy basis and funds were allotted accordingly. Even if members of this august House who are today in the ruling party deny this merely because they are in the ruling party, I wish to say that these things cannot but cast their shadows in their minds.

For example, in 1968-69 only Rs.6 crore 51 lakh was allotted for the welfare of the Scheduled Castes and backward classes together. Of that Rs.6 crore 51 lakh, if we ask how much was spent on backward-class people, it was only Rs.1 crore 73 lakh. I am not making a complaint that more was spent on Harijans and less on backward classes.

While still saying that more should be spent on Harijans, the Kazhagam Government, after 1969, proceeded with the objective that more should also be spent on the backward classes, whose population was greater. With the policy aim of working more extensively for the welfare of both the Scheduled Castes and the backward classes, it reorganised what had then been the Harijan Welfare and Backward Classes Welfare functions into separate departments—

### Source page 361

—and not only made them separate departments but entrusted them to separate Ministers. To say more, a Minister was created under the title “Minister for Backward Classes Welfare,” and some other departments and responsibilities were also entrusted to him. The country knows this well.

Along with separating the departments and appointing separate Ministers, a committee was constituted to look after backward-class welfare; its recommendations were accepted. In place of the Rs.1 crore 73 lakh allotted for backward-class welfare in 1969, nearly Rs.6 crore was allotted for their welfare in 1975-76. I wish to inform this House of that.

In 1975-76, Rs.11 crore was allotted for Scheduled Caste welfare. We should not forget that the Rs.17 crore which the Kazhagam Government then allotted for backward-class and Scheduled Caste welfare has today risen to Rs.25 crore. The reason I mention this is that the policy commitment of the Government, and of the Kazhagam that ran it, to the advancement of the Scheduled Castes and backward classes was proclaimed in 1969 and was followed by continuing achievements. We must not forget that the foundation for the progress and growth enjoyed by backward-class people in the country today was laid by the Kazhagam Government then.

The Kazhagam Government announced in the financial statement a scheme called the Manu Needhi Thittam in 1969. Under the principle that people's grievances should, as far as possible, be resolved on the spot, officials went out to the villages—

—and every year received tens of thousands of petitions and found remedies for them there and then.

Fortunately, though it was printed in 1977, it did not conceal any of the achievements of the Kazhagam Government up to March 1975-76. The present Chief Secretary himself wrote its foreword. Therefore, we must not forget that it was the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam Government that formulated the policy-based scheme of building free houses for Harijans and fishermen living in villages.

It was precisely to expand protected drinking-water facilities in villages, small villages and large towns alike that the Kazhagam Government created the Water Supply Board and carried out that work on an extensive scale. Ruling-party members and members of friendly parties have pointed to the many references to villages in our Finance Minister's statement and asked us how many times villages had been mentioned. I too wish to remind them.

In the 1971-72 financial statement, when I held responsibility, I wrote a line which I wish to recall: “Treating the provision of protected drinking water to the thousands of scattered villages and coastal settlements as a life-saving medicine for a disease-free life, this Government has created a drinking-water scheme to carry out that programme rapidly.” I had stated that even then.

When they say that those who were in Government earlier did not care for villages, I consider it my duty to cite here both the line I wrote in the 1971 financial statement and the efforts taken in connection with it.

I shall recall another line from the 1971-72 financial statement: “This Government, acting with an unwavering desire that rural people should, within a period of time, obtain the facilities and opportunities available in urban areas, will take steps to provide electric light and electricity facilities to all villages at the earliest.”

It was the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam that brought a law to make residential house sites the property of the occupants. Please consider whether that was for cities or villages. The number of families that benefited from that law was 1,28,000. Of those 1,28,000 families, three-fourths were farmers and Scheduled Caste people; the rest were backward-class people. When the law was enacted and the function to implement it was held at Thiruthuraipoondi, the late Communist leader, the respected Manali Kandasamy, said emotionally: “Harijan and backward-class people who for many years had no way even to sleep in a hut of their own will go home after this meeting tonight saying, ‘Ah! Today I am going to sleep in my own hut.’” I cannot forget those words. We must therefore not forget that the law making those house sites their own arose from the policy held in the heart of the Kazhagam.

Under Congress rule, the ceiling was fixed at 30 standard acres. It was reduced to 15 standard acres, and not only was it reduced: lands that had until then been surplus, lands that the previous Government had not acquired—nearly 30,000 acres—were acquired and distributed to eligible poor, landless farmers under the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam Government. I am duty-bound to remind the House of that.

Tamil Nadu, during Kazhagam rule, became the first State in India to plan that there should be no village without electricity and to carry out that scheme.

People will cite Haryana and say Haryana provided electricity to all its villages. But if we compare Haryana with our Tamil Nadu, Haryana is only about the size of Chingleput district in Tamil Nadu. We must not forget that. It may be correct to place Haryana first and Tamil Nadu second in the order of States, but the land area is not comparable. In Tamil Nadu, many times larger, all villages except perhaps another one thousand or one thousand five hundred were provided electricity; in particular, more than 20,000 Harijan colonies were electrified under the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam Government.
