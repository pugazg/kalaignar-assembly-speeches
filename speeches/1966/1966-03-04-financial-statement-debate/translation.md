## Kalaignar's Legislative Assembly Speeches on the Financial Statement

**Speech: 8**  
**Date: 4.3.1966**

**Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi:** Hon. Speaker, our Chief Minister has placed before this House the Financial Statement for 1966–67, and in the debate now taking place on it I too wish to put forward a few of my views. Members of the ruling party began by praising this Budget as a tax-free Financial Statement, and by the end of their speeches they had set out their respective grievances and pointed to the various schemes in their constituencies which, despite being raised so many times, have remained unimplemented and shelved.

There is nothing to rejoice about in calling this a tax-free Budget. Our Chief Minister, thinking that imposing taxes in a tax-free Financial Statement would become a major issue, very cleverly imposed all the taxes that had to be imposed two months earlier, secured the approval of the majority of this House, and now, while those taxes are in force, members of the ruling party argue that a tax-free Budget has been presented. That is indeed strange—

### Source page 91

**Hon. Mr. M. Bhaktavatsalam:** The argument is only that there are no further taxes.

**Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi:** The Chief Minister says there are no further taxes. It is like saying, “a gun without bullets.” Saying “there are no further taxes” is like that. A gun without bullets does not mean a gun into which no bullets were ever loaded. The bullets it had were used to hunt the poor and humble as though they were spotted deer; now the empty gun is being shown as a gun without bullets, and it is said that there are no further taxes. Taxes amounting to nearly Rs. 8 crores were imposed two months ago, and now the gun without bullets is shown with the claim that there are no further taxes.

At the same time, cannons are firing in the Central Government in the north. A tax on cotton cloth, tobacco tax, tax on diesel oil, sugar tax and many other such taxes are being loaded onto the heads of the people. At such a time, there is no basis whatsoever for rejoicing that this Budget contains no taxes.

The revenue of the Madras Government has increased this year by nearly Rs. 24 crores. The Budget itself tells us this. But did this Rs. 24-crore increase arise because socialism was put into practice? Or because privately owned factories were transferred to the public sector and income arose from that democratic-socialist upsurge? Or because the businesses of the big bus proprietors were nationalised and income was thereby created on socialist lines? No. It arose because an additional Rs. 8 crores in taxes had already been imposed—including increases in land tax and water tax—which members of the Congress party in this House themselves have asked to be withdrawn—

### Source page 92

—and even the President of the All India Congress, speaking at a meeting in Kudanthai, pointed out that tax relief should be given to farmers. I wish to draw the attention of this Cabinet to that.

Besides these Rs. 8 crores, we are in a position where we must bear Rs. 7 crores allocated by the Central Government, another Rs. 6 crores coming through sales tax, another Rs. 1 crore through motor-vehicle tax, and so on. Members of the ruling party said that because there was no tax, everyone in the Opposition had been disappointed; if there had been taxes, they would have spoken about all sorts of things, and therefore this had caused them a great disappointment.

Even if taxes are imposed, ruling-party members will welcome them and offer congratulations. Whether this Budget imposed taxes or not, they would still praise and congratulate it. So there is no disappointment on that account. Our disappointment is that we expected this Financial Statement to bring the happy news that at least one or two of the many taxes already imposed had been removed.

I do not know whether it is true or not, but when a camel is unable to walk because more and more weight has been loaded on it, they will unload one or two articles and try to make it walk. It is truly regrettable that even such an effort finds no place in this Budget.

(**Hon. Mr. M. Bhaktavatsalam:** We will not disappoint.)

The Chief Minister says they will not disappoint. Even a camel walks because the weight on it is reduced. Whether or not one examines the matter on the basis of whether the camel is being “disappointed,” the load is removed because one thinks it will then walk. But in this State—

### Source page 93

—the increases in land tax and water tax that have been imposed are cruel, unjust and capable of tormenting farmers. They can throw soil over the very lives of cultivators. This has been said not only from the Opposition side; progressive-minded people on the ruling and Opposition sides have said it as well. If, even after that, they remain stubborn, we may praise the Chief Minister's stubbornness, but we are being forced today to search and search for whether there is any compassion in him.

Before stating what the Opposition generally thinks of this Budget, I think it appropriate to place before this House the opinion expressed by the respected Dr. Natarajan of the ruling party in an article he wrote. In that journal the article appeared simply under the name “Dr. P. Natarajan”; even the connection “M.L.A.” was not added. Perhaps it appeared under “Dr. P. Natarajan” because of some thought as to whether it was proper to say such things while being an M.L.A. of the ruling party.

**Speaker:** He is so well known.

**Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi:** He writes, “This Budget has not given importance to the country's most important problem, the food problem.” He goes on to say: “At this critical stage of our economy, first priority must be given to increasing food production—

### Source page 94

—not only food grains, but also fish, poultry, vegetables and the like. Looking at the present allocation from this angle, in a Financial Statement with agricultural revenue of one crore rupees, the total amount allotted for this is only Rs. 9 crores—only 5½ per cent. The important item that must be considered under capital expenditure is irrigation. But even there the allocation is low. Capital expenditure on agricultural reform and agricultural research has also been greatly reduced.

“At a time when all our energies and resources should be directed towards increasing food production, insufficient importance has been given to food production. Considering the time and the need, the manner in which the Budget has been framed is not satisfactory.”

That is what he has written.

In this situation, many from the ruling party speak as follows. When the Opposition says prices have risen and the people are suffering from poverty, the ruling party replies that the people's purchasing power has increased; that the people of Tamil Nadu are living luxuriously; that one cannot find anyone without a wristwatch; that one cannot find anyone without a shirt, and so on.

But there are very many people in this country who do not possess enough means even to buy food. It is not I who say this. I wish to place before the House how movingly Dr. Gopalan, Director of the Nutrition Research Institute, has pointed this out:

“In the present condition of our agricultural economy, malnutrition in our country is caused by insufficient food supply and by the fact that a substantial section of our people does not have the money needed to buy the food it requires—

### Source page 95

.”

That is what he has stated. But what does our respected Chief Minister say? He recommends that on Thursday nights people eat something other than rice and wheat. In what situation? Dr. Gopalan movingly says that a substantial section of the people lacks the economic means to buy food. Yet at the same time our respected Chief Minister recommends that on Thursday nights people eat something other than rice and wheat. He recommends sago bagalabath, chickpea sundal, greens and the like. Dr. Gopalan says that a substantial section of the people does not even have the economic means to buy food.

**Hon. Mr. M. Bhaktavatsalam:** You have quoted Dr. Gopalan. I expressed my view in accordance with his view.

**Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi:** I mention it precisely on the basis that a substantial section of the people does not have the means to buy those things, and for no other reason.

The ruling party's claim that the standard of living has risen is like standing on the bank and clapping and laughing at a man who cannot swim, who has tried to wade through neck-deep water and is now floating away as a corpse, while admiring how great his ability to float is. Like clapping and laughing from the bank, this is how the ruling party speaks today while poor people, workers and ordinary people struggle to buy food—

.

On page 6 of this Budget it is stated: “We have controlled the rise in prices to some extent and have given some relief to urban people generally and, in particular, to those with fixed incomes.” To argue that prices have been controlled is like saying, “A white crow is flying upside down.” I wish to show with one or two examples how contrary to truth this is.

Let us see how much the prices of everyday cooking essentials have risen in this one-year period, between March 1965 and today in March 1966. In March 1965, thuvaram paruppu cost Re. 1 per litre; today it costs Rs. 1.20. Ulutham paruppu cost 90 paise per litre in March 1965; today it is Rs. 1.05. Payatham paruppu cost 90 paise per litre in March 1965; today it is Rs. 1.20. Chillies cost Rs. 2.32 per kilogram in March 1965; today Rs. 3.75. Coriander cost Rs. 2.40 per kilogram; today Rs. 3. Shikakai cost Rs. 1.50 per kilogram in March 1965; today Rs. 1.80. Tamarind cost Rs. 1.52 per kilogram in March 1965; today Rs. 2.70. It seems one may somehow buy even tamarind—buying tamarind itself has become so difficult. Gingelly oil cost Rs. 2.95 per kilogram; today Rs. 3.90. Groundnut oil cost Rs. 2.87; today Rs. 3.80. Coconut oil was Rs. 4 per kilogram in March 1965; today it is Rs. 6.20. If we take meat, in March 1965 it was Rs. 3 per kilogram—

### Source page 97

—and today it is Rs. 4. Eggs were 20 paise in March; now they are 25 paise.

If a man went to the shop in March 1965 with Rs. 20.56, to buy all the things I have listed he would today have to take Rs. 28.60. Take an ordinary family which, by God's grace, does not have too many children; take a household consisting of one man and one woman. The items I have listed alone cost Rs. 28. Apart from that, rice has to be bought, house rent paid, medical expenses met, laundry expenses, recreation expenses, good and bad occasions—all these have to be counted. Can an ordinary family live in peace today?

The rulers show only the statistics in the books. Do all these statistics appear to reflect the people of the country? You say, “Look at the books.” We ask you to look at the situation in the country. If you look at the great majority of ordinary families, is there peace anywhere? Is there any place without a sigh of distress?

Take the family of someone earning less than Rs. 100—Rs. 75 or Rs. 60. Even if we assume there are no children at all, if one man and one woman spend frugally—if they spend like our Government, they cannot run their life properly—can they at least somehow make ends meet? That itself is a question. In this situation, how can an ordinary family in this country live in peace? There is hardship for food. Prices keep rising like poison—

### Source page 98

. Even if we suppose that life can somehow be carried on without food, one feels compelled to ask whether even water to quench thirst is freely available under the rule of these charitable gentlemen. The drinking-water problem is rampant and flying its flag.

Why has no scheme been brought to supply Madras city with drinking water from the Cauvery, more than 200 miles away? Although the Government must bear full responsibility for this, because the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam sits in the administration of the Madras Corporation, imaginary reasons are being offered as though it alone were responsible. Others too ask the Corporation why this Cauvery scheme has not been brought. It is a fair question, but it is being asked in the wrong place.

Today, we and they together ask and press the Government. By implementing a scheme capable of bringing Cauvery water from 200 miles away, the drinking-water problem can certainly be tackled to a great extent.

That is the problem as far as Madras city is concerned. But in Tiruchirappalli the Cauvery and the Kollidam flow around the city, and still there is a shortage of drinking water. Fortunately, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam is not in the administration there; the Congress party runs it. A river flows nearby, and yet Tiruchirappalli continues to face a water shortage.

In Thanjavur, next to Tiruchirappalli, the population was 1 lakh 10 thousand in 1961; today it is 1 lakh 25 thousand. There is no water scheme corresponding to this increase in population. The amount of water considered necessary for the people there—

### Source page 99

—is 15 gallons per person per day; in the earlier period 10 gallons were supplied. Today even 6 gallons are not supplied properly. With the quantity falling like this, the people of Thanjavur are being pushed into a situation where one must ask whether they will be given enough “gallons” of water or whether “Kaalan” will come for them.

A Vennar scheme was drawn up. The present Palli Agraharam drainage near the intake is being blocked by silt from the source printed as “Pewari.” That too has not been attended to. A Rs. 30-lakh drinking-water scheme here is being kept safely in a box and worshipped. Though requests and appeals have been made for the scheme to be accepted immediately—and the municipal council there is itself run by the Congress party—even after they have taken it to the Government, it has not been implemented to this day.

When one says Thanjavur, writers beginning stories about it speak of a land rich in water and soil, the fertile Chola country. Yet in the capital of such a Thanjavur district, people are suffering for drinking water.

Through a question in this House I pointed out how severe the drinking-water problem is in Coimbatore. It was said that the problem continues because the Siruvani scheme has not been carried out. This Siruvani scheme lies two miles from the Tamil Nadu border and 22 miles from Coimbatore city. The area in which it lies belongs to Kerala. With Kerala's assistance, this Siruvani scheme must be implemented for Coimbatore. Because we have not been able to make it work to our advantage, the people of Coimbatore—

### Source page 100

—are suffering for drinking water today.

Earlier, 50 lakh gallons of water were supplied to Coimbatore city; now only 35 lakh gallons are supplied. It has been stated categorically that if this scheme is implemented, one and a half crore gallons will be available. Water from the Siruvani dam is not being used by Kerala either. Therefore we must make every effort to use it.

It was said that borewells would be sunk to overcome the present water shortage. Borewells are said to be sunk in many places, but party differences enter even there. In some places work is stopped because of party rivalry. Some borewells yield no water. Where water tanks exist, the Board does not come forward to supply electricity for pump sets. There is not even an engineer there. It is said that no one has been appointed for three months, that party politics is the reason, and that certain important people are interfering. If that is true, I request that appropriate action be taken.

In this situation, for the water supplied by lorries in Coimbatore, the councillor in each area officially signs under a ration-card system, and drinking water is supplied today to a family in quantities such as one pot or two pots.

There is no food, no clothing, no water to drink. If all these are absent, what is there in this Budget? Looking at it reminds me of the story of Tenali Raman—

### Source page 101

. A king gave Tenali Raman a house and gave him the materials needed to paint pictures on its walls. Tenali Raman took the purse of gold that had been given to him, went home and slept well. An hour before the king was due to come and see the paintings, he woke up, frightened: what was he to do, what answer would he give if the king arrived? He took a broom, dipped it in cow dung, and drew four or five lines here and there on the wall.

The king came as announced and asked, “Where is the painting?” Tenali Raman pointed to the lines. When the king asked what they were, he said, “This is the horse's tail.” “The tail is here; where is the horse?” the king asked. “The horse is behind the wall,” he replied.

Likewise, when we ask where the schemes for the people are in this Budget, they show us only the tail and say, “This is the scheme.” When we ask where the life that should come through the scheme is, they say it is on the other side. That is how this Budget has been presented.

They keep talking of plans and plans, stressing a planned economy, but what worthwhile scheme have you fought for and obtained for Tamil Nadu? Only yesterday, for the first time, our Minister raised our battle cry in Madras State. It is both highly amusing and one of the wonders of the world. Our Industries Minister Mr. Venkataraman even used the phrase “the narrow-mindedness of the Central Government.” He is a man who always speaks gentle words; anger hardly ever comes to him. If even such an Industries Minister—

### Source page 102

—has angrily said, with our Chief Minister sitting nearby, that schemes useful to us are not being given to us, we must think about it.

The Minister told journalists with delight that the Tuticorin thermal-power project had been accepted nearly 1¾ years ago. He said it was regrettable that even after a project accepted 1¾ years earlier, the Central Government had still not approved it and was not ready to take immediate action. I think it was the following week that Minister K. L. Rao, replying to a question in Parliament, spread out his hands and said, “Tuticorin? What Tuticorin? When did we approve it? There is nothing of the sort.”

After hearing that, Minister Venkataraman either called the journalists or they came to him—either way, it makes no difference—and told them: “Here is K. L. Rao's letter. Minister T. T. K., Planning Commission Chairman Mr. Ashok Mehta, and this same Mr. K. L. Rao all sat together; the Tuticorin thermal-power project was approved, a decision was taken to implement it, and the Tamil Nadu Council of Ministers, specifically the Minister concerned, was officially informed by letter.” He showed that letter to the journalists.

After all this, if the Tuticorin thermal-power project is still in doubt, what is the reason? The Tamil Nadu Government does not demand an answer firmly. It proceeds as though it expects favour—

### Source page 103

. We too pay taxes to them. The Tamil Nadu Government seems to have lost the feeling that there is nothing wrong in acquiring the voice to ask them with authority.

If they ask for sesame, they will not give oil. Only if they ask for oil will they at least give sesame. But when you go to ask, you ask only, “At least give us sesame.” If they say, “There is no sesame,” you say, “Never mind then.” Asking for sesame and unable to get even kollu, the State Government traps itself in difficulty and reduces itself to expressing anger only occasionally, in situations like this.

The Salem iron and steel plant has a ten-year history. It has been talked about since 1956–57. But what concrete result has emerged so far? Studies, studies, studies; tours, tours, tours; they come from there and go from here. What concrete result has emerged beyond this? Let me remind this House once of its brief history.

On 29 June 1957, Mr. Subramaniam, who was then seated here as Finance Minister, said: “The National Industrial Development Corporation is expected to establish a plant to smelt iron using Salem's raw mineral resources. The necessary tests are being conducted at Jamshedpur.”

That was what Mr. C. Subramaniam stated here in June 1957. In July 1957 he said: “Only if we produce plenty of iron in our State can we begin major industries. There is iron ore in Salem. During British rule it was said that because there was no coal, an industry could not be started using that ore—

### Source page 104

. Now lignite is available at Neyveli. The next thing will be the iron plant. It will certainly come. On the question of establishing an iron plant at Salem, it is not a matter of our saying ‘yes’ to the north because the north is an obstacle. Tests are being conducted at Jamshedpur to determine what kind of iron Salem has and what kind of industry can be started with it.” Mr. Subramaniam said this too.

Next, on 17.3.1960, our leader Anna, who was seated here, asked the Finance Minister: “Will the Salem iron plant come under the Third Plan?” Mr. Subramaniam replied: “If the test of whether this iron can be used with Neyveli coal succeeds, the iron plant will come in the Third Plan.”

Then, in 1961, Finance Minister Mr. Subramaniam stopped his answer at this point: “Looking at the statistics, confidence has arisen that iron can be produced at a somewhat lower price. The laboratory test has ended favourably. For the pilot test, 2,000 tons of coal are required.”

On 7.7.1962 I asked Minister Mr. Venkataraman: “What stage has the Salem iron plant reached? What is the result of the experts' investigation?” He replied: “The pilot test for Neyveli lignite and Salem iron ore is under way in East Germany. When the Laboratory Director expressed his view, he said he was confident it would succeed.” Minister Venkataraman said this with such happiness as to give everyone here confidence.

On 22.1.1964, Chief Minister Bhaktavatsalam, while answering a question, stated that how coal was to be used and how the project was to be started were under Government consideration, and that it was clearly evident the scheme was feasible.

On 23 January 1965, Minister Venkataraman, answering a question, said: “If the Central Government fails to take up and run the Salem steel plant, the State Government itself will take it up and run it.” In response to a question from the Hon. Member Mr. Krishnamurthi, the Minister said with confidence that even if the ruling party in the north did not help bring it about, “we ourselves will raise the necessary investment.” That answer is still fresh in memory.

When it was asked, “Then is all the money allocated for land acquisition being spent? What steps are being taken for land acquisition?”, the Minister replied: “All steps for acquiring the land are being taken; land acquisition itself takes at least nine or ten months.”

Further, when regret was expressed that the Salem steel plant had still not come, the Chief Minister said: “It was observed that the Governor's Address did not mention the Salem steel plant. Why was it not mentioned again? Once a view has been stated, it need be mentioned in the following year's address only if there is some change. In any case, the Salem steel plant will certainly come. As we said earlier, this will be a small project. Japanese experts have provisionally agreed that it is a feasible project. Their report has not yet come.” The Hon. Chief Minister stated this on 5.2.1966.

### Source page 106

He had said that they would mention it in such statements only if there was some change. Now it has been mentioned in the Budget. That has made all of us afraid. “They said they would mention it only if there was a change; now they have mentioned it—what will happen to the project, what is its position?” That fear has arisen.

What do they finally say about it? In the Budget speech, the Financial Statement speech, they state: “The detailed project report prepared by the Melars Dastur Company has already confirmed that the Neyveli–Salem steel-plant scheme is practicable both technically and economically. We expect the report of the Japanese consultancy study team to be finalised shortly. Thereafter the scheme will be taken up as early as possible.”

Thinking of these ten years brings a sigh. By the time the study for the Salem steel plant is completed, the Russians and Americans have conducted research in outer space and shown the world that people can go into space and meet one another there. Space research itself has succeeded, yet the study of the Salem steel plant is still not complete.

The Minister has now gone to Japan and returned. The last World War too ended in Japan. In the same way, I convey my wishes to the Minister that the struggle for the iron-smelting plant at Salem should also end with Japan.

This is the situation even while we have an able Minister, Mr. Venkataraman. We hear that he too is going north. If he leaves, the question arises whether Salem will be held up. He may go in the confidence that the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, which has more concern than we do, will come next time and take care of it—

### Source page 107

. If, however, you yourselves are going to return with a majority, then stay and complete it first, and after that go north, south, east or west wherever you like. If you are going to return with a majority, I wish to say that you yourselves should remain and finish it.

There has been no struggle over this. It has not been pressed with sufficient concern. Our abilities have not been used for it. As I have pointed out, the Central Government must be told the pitiful ten-year history and told that Tamil Nadu is seething over it.

I read that the Chief Minister spoke firmly and emphatically about this to the late Lal Bahadur. That firmness and emphasis must continue until the Salem steel plant comes to Tamil Nadu. We also read in the newspapers that Industries Minister Mr. Venkataraman has spoken of disputes over whether it should be five lakh tons or two and a half lakh tons. These matters must be resolved quickly and the work of bringing the Salem iron plant into being must be pursued.

I think I can give just one example of how Tamil Nadu's voice has not been properly carried and made heard there, and then quickly conclude my speech, judging by the Hon. Speaker's eyes.

Across the Indian subcontinent, demands are being made from several States that certain areas should be declared backward regions so that they may receive special concessions in agriculture, irrigation, industry, transport and education, and special plans may be drawn up for them. One such demand—

### Source page 108

—in Tamil Nadu is that areas such as Pudukkottai, eastern Ramanathapuram and Aranthangi should be declared backward regions.

On this demand, Mr. Umanath, a Member of Parliament and one of the leaders of the Left Communist Party, asked in Parliament: “Will Pudukkottai, eastern Ramanathapuram and Aranthangi be designated as backward regions in Tamil Nadu?”

The Planning Minister, Mr. Bhagat, then replied: “As far as Uttar Pradesh is concerned, some such places have been selected. A separate sum of Rs. 10 crores has been allocated in the Fourth Five-Year Plan so that those areas may receive special concessions.”

Following this, on 4.11.1965, two or three months ago, the same member asked another question. I do not know whether perhaps he sent it from prison. He asked, “Why are the Tamil Nadu areas not included in it?”

Mr. Bhagat replied: “The Uttar Pradesh Government wrote to me about its backward areas. Therefore the matter was considered and Rs. 10 crores has been allocated for them in the Fourth Five-Year Plan. But the Tamil Nadu Government has given us no information on this. If it does, we will consider it.”

Thus this is a fitting example showing that matters which ought to be conveyed to the Central Government are not being conveyed properly and at the proper time. Or, if they have been conveyed and something has happened in the intervening two months, I shall be happy. If they have not been conveyed—

—I request, on behalf of this House, that our Chief Minister and the other Ministers convey them.

One theme running through many places in this Financial Statement is economy. It says economy must be practised and efficiency must be practised. Though I have many notes and would like to explain the matter in detail, because there is no time I shall speak briefly.

If one reads the report of our Audit Committee, one can see clearly the extent to which economy has been ruined. I wish to mention only a few examples.

A scheme to build rental houses for low-paid Government employees was created in 1960–61. This Government entrusted the responsibility to the Housing Board after agreeing to meet the cost. It borrowed the required sum from the Life Insurance Corporation and gave it to the Housing Board. But the money was not spent immediately; only half of it was spent. The work remained undone up to 1964, while the Housing Board failed to carry it through. Because the departments concerned did not have the approach of obtaining the money at the time the work was actually to be done, Rs. 8 lakh 24 thousand was paid in interest alone.

Eight lakh rupees is not an ordinary sum. It could meet a portion of the Rs. 30 lakhs needed for the Thanjavur drinking-water scheme I mentioned. After wasting money in this manner, there is no meaning in speaking about economy.

An arrangement was made to spray pesticide by aircraft in the Villupuram area—

### Source page 110

. Rs. 10,000 was spent on preliminary work. After spending Rs. 10,000 on preliminary work, it was announced that the scheme had been abandoned because the Government of India was not prepared to send the aircraft. Could they not first have found out whether the aircraft would come before spending Rs. 10,000 on the preliminary work?

Next, as regards the Kendra Village Scheme, between 1961 and 1964 this Tamil Nadu Government opened 15 Kendra Villages. Up to 1964, the expenditure on Kendra Village staff allowance and breeding-bull maintenance was Rs. 18 lakh 38 thousand. The aim of the Kendra Village Scheme was to develop cattle through artificial insemination.

In the six Kendra Villages started in 1961–62, the number of cattle considered fit for breeding was 202,686. The number of calves expected from them was 81,074. But the calves actually obtained—poor things!—numbered 6,888.

In 1962–63, in six Kendra Villages, the number of cattle was 116,969. The expected number of calves was 46,787. The number actually obtained was only 784.

What reason does the department give for this failure? “Some cows were barren. Some were old.” Was this not known at the beginning? “During periods when they did not give milk, many cows were used for agricultural work. There was a shortage of fodder and no grazing ground was available. The Kendra Village areas were located in regions unsuitable for breed improvement.” These are the reasons given.

Because these things were not considered at the outset, Rs. 18 lakh 38 thousand was wasted. After such unjust waste, I wish to say there is no meaning in talking of economy, economy.

If economy is to be properly applied, our Ministers should come forward to reduce their own salaries to some extent. That would be a good example guiding others to practise economy—

### Source page 111

. I am not here to say how much they should reduce them. They too face today's high prices. The wife of our Home Minister, Hon. Mr. Kakkan, herself once stated at a conference: “Even though we receive Rs. 1,500, our family too is living amid many difficulties.” Therefore, if they themselves think it over and reduce their salaries to some extent, it may serve as an example to others.

Many lakhs of rupees are spent because there is a post called Governor. That too can be saved. If that post were abolished, it would also be a good example of economy.

Ministers too can reduce their tours to some extent. Saying, “We are meeting the people,” is not a sound argument. I am not the one saying this. Mr. Kamaraj, who brought the Kamaraj Plan, himself said: “I cannot meet the people while being a Minister. When I go out saying I must meet the people, large Nandis stand in the way in the villages. That is why I am resigning.” This programme of economy can also be carried out by reducing ministerial tours.

In addition, if Ministers resign their posts about three months before the 1967 general election, the salaries paid to Ministers can thereby be saved, and democracy too can be protected. It is only three months; nothing more is needed. They too may have a desire: whether we return next time or not, let us remain for those three months—

### Source page 112

. Even so, if they resign for those three months and enter the general election, our respected Ministers—who take pride in saying that the Tamil Nadu Government has been the cause and the beginning of many things that made democracy blossom in the Indian subcontinent—should do this as well and stand as a good precedent.

It is only because proper remedies have so far not been provided in this Budget for all the matters I have raised here that I have had to point them out. I request that this Cabinet make a determined effort, at least during the one year remaining to it, to find remedies for them.

Otherwise, this Cabinet is approaching the point at which, in 1967, it will have to answer for the cruelties experienced during eighteen years of rule, for the sufferings and distress of the poor, for the tears shed because of poverty, and for the sighs they have heaved. I request that, during the intervening period, it at least undertake these tasks so that it may earn the name that it did this much. With that, I conclude my speech. Thank you.
