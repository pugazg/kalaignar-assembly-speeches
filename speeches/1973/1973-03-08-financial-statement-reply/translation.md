## In the Legislative Council

**Hon. Dr. M. Karunanidhi:** Honourable Chairman of the Council, on the Financial Statement placed before this House, 42 members, including the Honourable Leader of the Opposition, have expressed their views. Of the 62 members in this House, 42 have expressed their views, and finally the Leader of the Opposition too has set out here many good suggestions and the conditions prevailing in the country.

When the Leader of the Opposition began his speech, he said that when this Financial Statement was placed there was a peaceful atmosphere in Tamil Nadu, but that over the intervening two days a disturbed situation had arisen because of the teachers' agitation. He certainly knows all the facts about this teachers' agitation. Yesterday our friend Arumugasamy, while speaking, said one thing. He said that whether they belong to the Opposition parties or to the ruling parties, they must work to educate the people and explain good things to them. Judging by his speech yesterday, it would not be an exaggeration to say that he examined certain problems almost from the position of Thiru Jayaprakash Narayan, without speaking either on behalf of the party ruling India or on behalf of the Opposition here. Our Leader of the Opposition, though he knew all the true particulars of the teachers' agitation—

—even then said that the Chief Minister and the Education Minister should speak with the true representatives of the teachers, which amounted to saying that those carrying on the agitation .........

**Thiru K. Rajaram Naidu:** It should not be taken in a wrong sense, Chief Minister. I said that they should send those whom they themselves accept as their representatives.

**Hon. Dr. M. Karunanidhi:** I am mentioning this only so that what he said should not create the mistaken impression that perhaps we—the Education Minister and I—did not speak to the true representatives. Those who met me and the Education Minister on the 5th were the president and the general secretary of the Graduate Teachers' Association, together with a few of its office-bearers. I do not know who could be truer representatives of the Graduate Teachers' Association than these. The irony of the present situation is this: if demands are not fulfilled, an agitation taking place is one thing. But the unfortunate situation in our Tamil Nadu is that even after the demands have been fulfilled, even after they have been announced in a great and honoured House like this, even after it has been stated firmly, in the presence of the most distinguished elders in this House, that the necessary orders will be issued, the teachers' association puts out reports saying, "Our six demands have not been settled, have not been settled"; and good people in the country express no view at all about this and remain silent in the thought that, if this agitation takes place, let it become a kind of political gain. I submit with great humility that this is not conducive to the future good of the country of which our Leader of the Opposition spoke.

If, even after some good measures have been carried out, an agitation is conducted, then there are those who are instigating these teachers, or those who have somehow entered the teachers' association and wish to seek publicity through it, or those who wish to attract these teachers towards themselves and pull them to their side.

### The Yama who danced

This is what it is like. Yesterday I was reading the book *My Life in Drama*, written by the late artiste T. K. Shanmugam, who was once in our House. At one point he has recorded an incident. About forty years ago, when he was very young, he went with that drama troupe to Mallangkinaru, near Virudhunagar, to perform a play. He was then so young that he could play the role of Narada. There were no electric lights in that village. They staged the play with hurricane lamps and Petromax lamps, before a very large crowd. He has also described how the crowd came. Instead of printing posters or distributing handbills, at eight o'clock at night they would set off an explosive in a public place in the village. When the "padak!" sound was heard, people in the neighbouring villages would know that a play was being staged there and would gather.

### Is there no scope for taxation?

When our friend Sainathan spoke on this Financial Statement, he said that one could not rejoice merely because a tax-free Budget had been prepared. The leaders of the Opposition said the same thing. It is not wrong, whether on behalf of the ruling party or—

### Source page 45

—on behalf of the allied parties, to welcome the fact that this Budget has been presented without taxes. At the same time, merely because a tax-free Financial Statement has been given this year, it is not right to say that we shall never impose a tax again. Sainathan said there was no room at all for taxation. If the Finance Department and the other departments look a little more deeply and begin to search, it is not as though no room can be found. But I would make it clear that this Government will impose a tax only after seeing whether the sources thus found are capable of bearing that burden.

### Per capita income

He gave a calculation. Per capita income is Rs. 397. Earlier it was Rs. 370. At that time, the tax per head was Rs. 39. Now, he said, it has become Rs. 70 or 75. This is only a multiplication-and-division calculation. When per capita income is said to be Rs. 397, we cannot take it to mean that every one of the four crore people in Tamil Nadu earns Rs. 397. Take me, Thiru Swaminathan, and another ordinary person. If my income is Rs. 20, Swaminathan's income is Rs. 10, and the other person's income is Rs. 3, then 20 + 10 + 3 = 33 and the figure for the three of us is Rs. 11. That figure may be Rs. 9 lower in my case and Rs. 1 lower in Swaminathan's; but it does not mean that the person earning Rs. 3 has become Rs. 8 richer. In the same way, even if it is said that the tax per head has now risen to Rs. 75, just as the figure of Rs. 397 in per capita income does not belong to each one of the four crore people, we must understand that this tax too does not fall on each one of the four crore people. I wish to point that out at this time.

Our friend Damodaran, the elder Krishnaswami Naidu, and our Leader of the Opposition asked: if you have prepared a tax-free Budget, why could you not have removed the Bangladesh tax? Even when we imposed the Bangladesh tax, we did not specifically describe it as a Bangladesh tax. Anticipating beforehand that a time like this might arise, we imposed it generally for defence and development works—

—so defence is still needed now, and development works are still needed. In this State, in Mysore State and in Punjab State, this tax continues. In Punjab they have altered it into an Educational Cess, an education cess. In Rajasthan they have altered it into a drought tax. Some people asked: the Central Government has removed the Bangladesh tax; why has it not been removed here? If it is to be removed in the same manner in which the Central Government removed it, I am prepared to remove it here too. There they imposed more than Rs. 290 crore in taxes and then removed the Bangladesh tax. If this House permits it here too, we can introduce another tax of that kind and then remove the Bangladesh tax.

For the present, the Honourable Leader of the Opposition said that a share of it has to be given to the Central Government, and asked why it should not therefore be removed. In response to their request, this Government has decided that, instead of giving that share to the Central Government, we ourselves will add that amount and remove it from next year. That alone I am obliged to state.

### Who is causing confusion?

At this time, when you say that the Opposition too should have a responsibility to guide the people without making them subject to confidence and panic, our Leader of the Opposition—the leader of the old Congress Party—may ask whether that responsibility belongs only to the ruling party; the leader of the Swatantra Party may ask it; the leader of the Muslim League may ask it; Thiru A. R. Damodaran and Thiru Raja Iyer may ask it. But I say this with great pain. At a time when conditions here are gradually returning to normal, Ministers at the Centre are suddenly saying one thing or another that tends to confuse the minds of the people.

Thiru Mohan Kumaramangalam came here. Coal was asked for, and he said, "I will send it." It is certainly to his credit to say, "They asked; I sent it." He could have stopped there. Instead he leaves after giving out the news: "I read in the newspapers that Tamil Nadu needs coal. I immediately ordered it sent."

What do the people think? They think, "This Government has taken no responsibility of any kind; our Thiru Mohan Kumaramangalam saw the report in the newspaper and, with such generosity, did this." Thiru Mohan Kumaramangalam gains credit. Even if we suffer loss of prestige, I am not worried about that. But if people get the false impression that this Government has paid no attention at all to a major problem and that a Central Minister had to learn about it from the newspaper—

—and if those who seek political gain from that begin to move, then conditions that damage law, order and peace in the country arise of themselves.

Even after I issued a denial about it both in the Legislature and in the Assembly, no correction or explanation has so far come from the Centre. One day, all of a sudden, Central Minister Thiru C. Subramaniam said that the handloom yarn allotted for Tamil Nadu had still not been taken by them. Our Industries Minister Thiru Madhavan replied in the Assembly that there was nothing of the kind: they had not allotted it, nor had we refused to take it. I have given an explanation about it here as well. Even after that, did they issue any correction or explanation? No.

### Can one divine the future?

This morning, looking at the newspapers, even the leaders of the Opposition referred to it; I think they did not refer to it fully, but they too mentioned it. What does our respected Thiru C. Subramaniam say in Parliament? He says that if the Samayanallur thermal power station had not been disposed of, these conditions would not have arisen. Those in the Opposition can sometimes be in a position where they do not know the details. But when a person in the ruling party—indeed, a Minister, and one who had been a Minister in Tamil Nadu—says something in Parliament, if I suspect that he is saying it deliberately, or saying it to seek political gain for his party here, that is not my fault; it is his fault and no one else's. He says, "They sold the Samayanallur thermal power station. They should have anticipated this situation." The tenders for that thermal power station at Samayanallur were finally settled in February 1972. We are now in March 1973. Almost a full year has passed—

### Source page 50

—since then. In February 1972, could we have divined that there would be a shortage of rain like this, that the south-west monsoon would fail us, that therefore we would not get water in hydro-electric stations such as Kundah, and that in Kundah, where many thousands of lakh cubic feet of water ought to be available, only 1,000 or 1,500 lakh cubic feet would be available? Could we have told fortunes? If so, should we not have said it beforehand? And even if such a prediction had been made, could one have placed faith in it?

### Not 30 megawatts: 14 megawatts

Something unexpected occurred. The Samayanallur thermal power station was incurring an annual loss of Rs. 55 lakh. In 1968, an expert committee was appointed to examine what should be done with it. That expert committee suggested that it could be closed. An unavoidable Rs. 55 lakh was being spent; the maximum available there was 14 megawatts; and that 14 megawatts of electricity meant only one lakh units a day. For Tamil Nadu, which requires 225 lakh units, that one lakh units a day could not be treated as significant. This was something that happened a year ago. No other wrongdoing took place. To say even more, there was not even a desire to give it to a private party.

In 1970 we agreed to give it to the Electricity Board of the composite Punjab State. They too agreed to take it. At first we concluded the talks at Rs. 40 lakh and asked them to take it for Rs. 42 lakh. From the Punjab Electricity Board, on 9—7—1970, they said:

> ‘The offer of Rs. 45 lakhs was had on behalf of the Board. However as a matter of compromise, an agreed figure of Rs. 42 lakhs has been adopted subject to confirmation by the Boards on either side’

The Punjab Electricity Board wrote this in its letter of 9—9—70. Later, on 6—8—71, they themselves wrote another letter. In that letter:

> ‘I regret to say that the Board gave a second look to your offer and decided not to proceed with it further’

### Commission of Inquiry

Our Leader of the Opposition, who spoke last in this House, and some of the honourable members who spoke yesterday—the leader of the Tamil Arasu Kazhagam and Deputy Chairman of this Council, Thiru M. P. Sivagnanam, our friend Thiru Eeswaramoorthy, and Thiru Swaminathan—spoke here about the Commission of Inquiry. The Leader of the Opposition expressed many views about it; Thiru Swaminathan did so too. The first thing I wish to ask them is this: should not all these views be taken and stated in that committee constituted under the chairmanship of Thiru Madhavan? That is my first question. Do not answer now. I say it only so that you may think about it yourselves.

They say they did not merely say that the Bill was unwanted; they say they opposed it because, if a charge is not proved, there is a provision for three years' punishment. Sitting in the Select Committee, everyone has the right to say what should be retained and what—

### Source page 52

—should be left out. Rather than walking out of the Select Committee, if what they say is not accepted there, the Leader of the Opposition and the members also have the right afterwards to record their dissent. Without carrying out that course, if one simply says, “This is a black Bill, this is the Rowlatt Act,” people will think, “Oh! So this, perhaps, is what the Rowlatt Act is.” As the Honourable Member Arumugasamy said, we must educate the people. If this Bill is called the Rowlatt Act, then people who have never read about the Rowlatt Act, when they ask what it was, will think, “Perhaps appointing a judicial Commission of Inquiry like this is what the Rowlatt Act means.” They will misunderstand and think that perhaps this was the kind of law enacted in those days. So we must educate the people correctly.

### Do you know that law?

I say this because among those who rejected this Bill and walked out are members of the ruling Congress as well. It is the ruling Congress that is in power in Rajasthan and in Maharashtra. In the Bill introduced here in our Tamil Nadu, it is not even merely in the form they describe as “if the offence is proved.” Under the heading *Punishment for false, frivolous, fictitious complaint*:

> ‘notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, every person who makes a false, frivolous or fictitious complaint against public men under this Act shall on conviction be punished with imprisonment for a term which may extend to three years and shall also be liable to fine. —that is what we have stated in this draft law before our Select Committee. In Rajasthan, the ordinance of the ruling Congress, under the heading “First Published in Rajpatra”—“First Published in ‘Rajpatra’”—‘Raj Patra’—is said to begin so—Extra Ordinary Part 4, dated 25-1-1973; there too, in Section 13, this is what it says:

> **Prosecution for false complaint.—** ‘Notwithstanding anything contained in Section 10 or any other provision of this Ordinance, every person who willfully or maliciously, makes any false complaint under this Ordinance shall on conviction be punished with imprisonment for a term which may extend to three years and shall also be liable to fine’

That is how it stands in the law in Rajasthan. The same provision has a place in the law in Maharashtra too. The curious thing is that, in the laws in Maharashtra and Rajasthan, Ministers too are made subject to complaints of this kind. Even if an inquiry is conducted against them, charges may be brought against them. But:

### Anna's reply

But others ask why we should not submit ourselves to the Commission of Inquiry set up at the Centre. This debate is not of today or yesterday. As early as 1968, when Thiru Chavan was the Home Minister, he wrote a letter to Anna. In that letter he said that an institution called a ‘Lok Pal’ should be established there, that all the States should support it, and that if complaints were made against Ministers in the States, that Central institution would have the right to inquire into them. He wrote to our Chief Minister Anna and to the Chief Ministers of the other States. At that time Anna's view was:

> ‘Constitutionally, the Central Ministers are under the control of the Prime Minister and the State Ministers are under the control of the Chief Minister. The Chief Minister is independent of the Prime Minister and the Union whole Ministers. So, the Chief Minister will be the authority of the State Ministers’

Perarignar Anna stated this emphatically to Thiru Chavan at that time. Not only that: today the Swatantra Party people have all joined together and say that you must place yourselves within the Commission of Inquiry set up by the Central Government. Even saying that may be one thing. But to those who say, “Let the Central Government itself appoint a Commission of Inquiry against these people,” I say this: do you know what Thiru Singh Deo, who had been the Swatantra Party Chief Minister in Orissa, wrote to Chavan about this ‘Lok Pal’?

> ‘It may not be keeping with the demands of the Constitutional propriety that the observance of the Code of conduct by the Chief Minister should be entrusted to the Prime Minister and the Union Home Minister. In order to develop proper democratic practices, the State Legislature should be the authority for enforcing their observance of the code of conduct by the Chief Minister in the same way as the Parliament is the authority for ensuring the observance of the Code of conduct by the Prime Minister’

### Source page 55

That is to say, it would not accord with the fundamentals of the constitutional arrangement for the responsibility of supervising a State Chief Minister's observance of the code of conduct to be assumed by the Prime Minister of the country or by the Home Minister. Just as Parliament has the responsibility of watching whether the Prime Minister of the country acts without departing from proper conduct, so too the responsibility and authority for watching whether the Chief Minister of a State acts without departing from the code of conduct belongs to that State Legislature. Former Orissa Chief Minister Thiru Singh Deo wrote to Chavan that this would be conducive to the growth of the best democratic practices.

After that Anna convened a meeting of the State Cabinet, took a decision there, and communicated it to Chavan:

> ‘The Council have examined the suggestion in detail and have decided not to agree to it on various grounds including the ground that the institution of the Lok Pal have contemplated the infringe on the State autonomy guaranteed by the Constitution.’

That is to say, a guarantee has been given. This is incompatible with the State's autonomous character. Therefore, Anna wrote to Thiru Chavan on 2-6-1968 that he could not accept it. In that letter he said:

> ‘We feel that the setting up of a Lok Pal have contemplated by the A.R.C. would infringe on State autonomy as envisaged under the Constitution and as such we would advice against it’

That same position, and no other, is what this Government has followed from 1968 until now. If, imagining that we are acting out of fear of an inquiry, complaints against me or the other Ministers are taken there and the Central Government immediately appoints a Commission of Inquiry, I am truly pained. Today the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam is governing here. Tomorrow, if the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam is not here and another party governs, and likewise another party is in power at the Centre, then if the party at the Centre wishes to bring down the party governing the State, it will have obtained a very easy weapon. What need they do—

—? They need only catch hold of four people. I will appoint a Commission of Inquiry.” As soon as it is appointed, the Ministers will resign. Once the Ministers resign, the MLAs there will scatter. Then it will become like Orissa, or like some other State. Then they will say that President's Rule must come there.

Therefore, I say that such authority must not be placed in the hands of the Central Government, enabling it to topple, disrupt and destroy any State Government it dislikes.

It need not be a Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam Government. Even if the Communist Party comes to power tomorrow—fortunately, that will not happen—even if it does, or if any other party comes to power, when a party opposed to the Central Government is governing a State, if those at the Centre, with authoritarian thoughts under a democratic cloak—the Prime Minister and others—decide that it must be toppled, they can catch four people in that State, have them lodge a complaint, institute an inquiry, cause the Ministers to resign on that basis, create an upheaval there, and bring that Government down.

It is in this manner that today we have brought this draft law for establishing that Commission of Inquiry. ### Our opposition

A controversy arose here over agricultural income tax. Thiru Swaminathan, Thiru Narayanasamy, and our Leader of the Opposition who has just spoken all referred to it. I have already expressed the view that this agricultural income tax may affect the farmers of Tamil Nadu. At the Chief Ministers' Conference held in Delhi too, on behalf of the State I expressed my opposition to their including agricultural income for taxation.

Even after that, we see that agricultural income tax has now been introduced. Since there is a fear that it may affect the incentive of farmers here, perhaps the Central Government may abandon it. If it does not, then when we ask the Central Government as a matter of right, we must say: it is only by taking account of the agricultural income here that you increase your tax; you increase your revenue! To give an example, under the agricultural income-tax arrangement now introduced, a person whose present non-agricultural income is Rs. 5,000 pays no Central tax however much agricultural income he may receive. There is no tax for the Central Government. Whatever agricultural income the farmer receives, there is tax for the State Government. But if the non-agricultural income is only Rs. 5,000—

### Source page 58

—there is no need for him to pay tax to the Centre on account of income from agriculture. But if it is Rs. 6,000, tax must be paid to the Central Government. If non-agricultural income is Rs. 6,000, tax must be paid. On Rs. 6,000, Rs. 110 has to be paid as income tax. If agricultural income of Rs. 5,000 is added to that Rs. 6,000, making Rs. 11,000, the slab system operates and Rs. 187 must be paid as tax. A person with Rs. 6,000 of non-agricultural income had paid Rs. 110 in tax. If he now has Rs. 5,000 of agricultural income, the person who paid Rs. 110 must pay Rs. 187. Thus he must pay Rs. 77 more in tax. Since this is a tax imposed on the basis of agriculture here, we, on behalf of the Tamil Nadu Government, have a duty to press the Central Government: take this into account and give that Rs. 77 to Tamil Nadu. I firmly believe that the Central Government too will bear in mind that it is because of this that 110 becomes 187, and will take the necessary action.

### The Theosophical Society matter

While Thiru Vasanthabai was speaking, he referred to the Theosophical Society. The land already held by that Society was 296 acres. In January 1971, the State Government approached it so that the Housing Board could take 106 acres out of those 296 acres and build houses through the South Chennai Housing Board. The members of the Theosophical Society refused to give that land. After they refused, while the State Government had begun considering the matter, what they did was this—and what happened then was very troubling. Having refused to give it to the Housing Board, what did they do in the meantime? In Survey Nos. 56-1 and 56-2, they divided land into 191 grounds, with plots ranging from 1½ grounds to 3 grounds, and sold them, at Rs. 8,000 per plot, to a private concern in Chennai called Chennai Real Estate, executing a deed of sale. The Housing—

### Source page 59

—Board was taking steps to acquire it; in order to escape that acquisition, between 4—6—1971 and 8—6—1971 they hurriedly executed a deed in favour of the private Real Estate concern, and we came to know of it through the Collector. If evidence is needed as to whether this is true, the people belonging to that Real Estate concern made a representation to the Government in July 1972. What was their representation? They said that the Housing Board intended to acquire the Society's land; that they had bought 191 grounds in it; and asked the Government to leave that portion out and acquire the rest. ### Dindigul election

Finally, they spoke about Dindigul. No date has been fixed for the Dindigul election. At the very beginning all the parties said that the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam would be destroyed in Dindigul, would be defeated, would be overthrown. Thiru Swaminathan of the Swatantra Party is upset that I did not mention him. “It will lose, it will lose,” they said. Then, after some days, after the excitement of ten days had subsided, they said that wrongful votes had been entered in Dindigul.
