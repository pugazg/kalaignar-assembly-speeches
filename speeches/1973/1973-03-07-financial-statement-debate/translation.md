Speech 12 — Date: 07.03.1973

**Hon. Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi:** Hon. Deputy Speaker, eighty-one members, including Mr Ponnappa Nadar, leader of the Congress Party, have expressed their views on the financial statement placed before this House. Of the 175 members of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, 38 members, and of the 58 members belonging to parties other than the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, 43 members, took part in this debate and explained many valuable views here in accordance with the policies of the parties they respectively uphold. Whether those views were favourable or adverse, in the belief that all of them will help the wheel of this administration turn properly, I first convey my thanks and appreciation to the members of all parties who expressed their views.

Ordinarily, the debate on the Governor's Address begins after a member of the ruling party moves a motion of thanks. A debate on the financial statement of the Chief Minister or Finance Minister ordinarily begins with the opposition speaking against it first. But, for the first time, I think, in this House, this debate began neither on behalf of the ruling party nor, conversely, on behalf of the opposition,

<!-- source-page: 192 -->

but on behalf of an allied party. I am very happy to see that, right at the outset, the debate began on the footing that there was no opposition to it. Hon. member Mr Wahab of the Muslim League supported this financial statement, offered his good wishes, and opened the debate.

The party leaders and some members who spoke here, including my dear friend and leader of the Communist Party of India, K.T.K. Thangamani, said that they had been prevented from expressing their views on this financial statement. When that was said on behalf of the Communist Party, I understood it. When Mr Hande spoke on behalf of the Swatantra Party, I understood it. When others, apart from Mrs Anandanayaki, spoke on behalf of the ruling Congress Party, I understood it. But when Mr Ponnappa Nadar of the old Congress Party and a few others spoke in that manner, I did not understand it. The Swatantra Party, the Communist Party and the ruling Congress Party here are not parties that have so far borne responsibility for governing Tamil Nadu. But the Congress Party of our Mr Ponnappa Nadar ruled Tamil Nadu for twenty years. Mr Ponnappa Nadar, leader of our Organisation Congress, was among those who supported the holding of the Assembly here after I had been locked up for sixty-two days in Palayamkottai prison. When our Hon. Education Minister, Navalar Nedunchezhiyan, had been convicted and was in Chennai prison, he asked for one day's leave so that he could come out, assume the chairmanship of the Public Accounts Committee which he headed, and conduct its proceedings. Mr Ponnappa Nadar supported the attitude of the then old Congress Government which refused that permission. When he spoke about this, I found it amusing.

<!-- source-page: 193 -->

But what has actually happened? There is a case against Mr Thangamani. I do not wish to enter into the merits of that case, because it has gone before the court. Mr Hande asked why the Government could not withdraw it. That is a different issue. But the condition imposed on him by the court is that he may stay in Chennai. I do not understand how an order permitting him to stay in Chennai prevents him from attending the Assembly. The Assembly is meeting in Chennai itself. If, as happened once before, the Assembly were sitting in Ootacamund and Mr Thangamani had been ordered to remain in Chennai, there might be justification for lamenting, “Alas! They have prevented him from attending the Assembly.” The Legislature is meeting in Chennai. Yesterday, or perhaps the day before, my friend Mr Hande showed me the letter from our friend Thangamani which he read out today, and asked: “He wants to come to the Assembly; if he seeks bail, why should he not be released?” I immediately told him: “You know that it is not in my hands. It is a matter before the court. But I will tell you one thing. The order given to him says that he may stay in Chennai. There is no bar on his attending the proceedings of the Assembly.” He asked, “Oh, is that so?” and went away reassured. When speaking here he had to speak as he did, and he did so. But when Mr Ponnappa Nadar repeated many of the points made from the opposition benches, I was genuinely surprised.

Even now, not many days have passed. This House is likely to continue sitting until the end of this month and even into the first week of April. Mr K.T.K. Thangamani can have an opportunity to participate in it.

Some Hon. members who spoke on this financial statement differed somewhat from an important point I had underlined. In particular, Mr Senti Sundarabharathi and some other members, through their differing views, rejected my suggestion that the Finance Commission should occupy the principal place in providing finance to the States. Mr Senti Sundarabharathi said here that a policy had been stated that allocation of funds to the States should be made through the Finance Commission, and that this did not appear to him to be a sound policy. My explanation to them is simply this: in the financial statement I said that the Finance Commission should occupy the principal place in arranging the means by which State Governments obtain finance from the Central Government. I did not say that the Finance Commission itself should calculate and recommend expenditure on Plan schemes, nor that the Planning Commission should have no role whatsoever in allocating funds.

<!-- source-page: 195 -->

Our view is that the Planning Commission may be entrusted with responsibility for giving assistance to the States for annual Plans in accordance with the principles of the Finance Commission. That is what I pointed out in this statement. It is not my argument that the Planning Commission should have no role of any kind in allocating funds.

At present, the State Government's share of Central taxes and the lump-sum grants given by the Finance Commission are provided in accordance with the Finance Commission's recommendations. We say that this should continue. The State Government has the right to spend these as it chooses. But, with regard to the amount which the Planning Commission allocates for State Plan expenditure—and I think it is useful for everyone to know this—of the amount allocated by the Planning Commission for State Plan expenditure, 70 per cent is given as loan assistance and only 30 per cent as grant. What I say is that, as far as this grant assistance is concerned, the Planning Commission should give it to the States in accordance with the general recommendations of the Finance Commission. This is also the view of the Central Administrative Reforms Commission. As for Plan loan assistance, it is the view of this Government that an All India lending institution should be established to provide loans for large specified Plan works, with representation for the States in that institution.

We have only said that assistance for Centrally Sponsored Schemes—works carried out by the State on behalf of the Central Government—and assistance for schemes initiated by the various departments of the Central Government but implemented in the State by the State Government should all be brought within the recommendations of the Finance Commission. I did not say that everything

must be provided by the Finance Commission itself or that the Planning Commission should perform that entire function.

Many Hon. members referred in their speeches to certain doubts that had arisen in their minds regarding this financial statement. Mrs Anandanayaki pointed out one matter. She said: “Under the head ‘Public Health’, the budget allocation is shown as Rs.12.45 crore, while the revised estimate of expenditure is shown as Rs.10.04 crore. It has gone down.” I thank her for having noticed and pointed it out. But I am duty-bound to explain it to her. Of the Rs.12.45 crore allocated in the budget, Rs.3.86 crore was allocated for the Family Welfare Scheme. If that is excluded, only Rs.8.59 crore was allocated for Public Health. The Rs.10.04 crore now shown does not include expenditure on the Family Welfare Scheme.

**Mrs T.N. Anandanayaki:** At the same time, if we look at the next page, Rs.6 crore has been spent on the Family Welfare Scheme. How did that arise?

**Hon. Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi:** That is precisely what I am saying. Because Rs.6 crore has been shown separately for the Family Welfare Scheme, it has not been included here. We can therefore see that the Public Health allocation has risen from Rs.8.59 crore to a revised estimate of Rs.10.04 crore. Taken together, for the three departments of Medicine, Public Health and Family Welfare, the total budget estimate for 1972-73 was

<!-- source-page: 197 -->

Rs.34.69 crore. The revised estimate has risen to Rs.38.9 crore. This can be seen clearly if one looks at the book titled Budget Details.

Mr Hande made another similar point. He said that the Plan amount spent on agriculture was declining, and cited the figures shown there. He pointed out that the 1972-73 budget allocation was Rs.619 lakh and that the 1972-73 revised estimate was Rs.564 lakh. There are two components here: Plan expenditure and non-Plan expenditure. Whenever we discuss the financial statement, we have repeatedly said that this confusion should be removed, and that Plan and non-Plan expenditure should be shown together. Of this Rs.619 lakh, the Plan expenditure is Rs.564 lakh. At the same time as Plan expenditure has declined, substantial amounts have been allocated to the Agriculture Department through non-Plan expenditure. Along with this Rs.619 lakh of Plan expenditure, the 1972-73 budget allocation was only Rs.20.16 crore, but the revised estimate is Rs.24.18 crore. Therefore, it has not declined. If one looks only at Plan expenditure, what Mr Hande said would appear correct. But when Plan and non-Plan expenditure are considered together, I wish to point out that the amount we spend on the Agriculture Department as a whole has not declined.

Mr Kasiraman of the ruling Congress Party made one point. Though he is not here, because what he said appeared in the newspapers I have a duty to answer it.

<!-- source-page: 198 -->

The point he cited was this: in 1966-67, land revenue, irrigation tax, cess and agricultural income tax together yielded a total of Rs.840 lakh to this Government. This year too, the amount the Government will receive under the same heads is Rs.840 lakh. Therefore, he said, there has been no change, no tax has been reduced, and so to say that land revenue has been reduced is a mere “myth”. To explain this, we must not forget the changes we have made in agricultural income tax. After we announced that there would be no basic land assessment on wet or dry land up to five acres, we introduced agricultural income tax for well-to-do farmers cultivating cash crops, and through that we obtain somewhat more revenue. That too must be taken into account.

Nor is that alone sufficient. In assessing land revenue, instead of looking only at how much is obtained each year, one must calculate the average obtained over each five-year period. That is because, in some years, remissions of tax or instalment arrangements may come into play. If we therefore take a five-year period and look at the average, for the five years ending with 1966-67—the period cited by Mr Kasiraman—the average annual revenue under land tax was Rs.8 crore. Similarly, for the five years ending with 1973-74, the average annual land-revenue income is Rs.6.3 crore. Thus it has declined by about Rs.2 crore. Because the basic land tax was abolished, ordinary farmers have benefited from these concessions. As time goes on, the amount received through this

<!-- source-page: 199 -->

tax can only decline; there is no scope for it to increase, and I wish to make that clear.

Mr Gopal of the Cumbum constituency, speaking on behalf of our old Congress Party, made one point. He said, “In 1965-66, Rs.6.56 crore was allocated for industrial development; in 1973-74 only Rs.4.48 crore has been allocated.” If he looks at page 37 of the Budget Memorandum, he can understand one thing. Under Demand No. 35, “Industries”, the amount allocated for 1965-66 was Rs.6.56 crore. In the coming year, under the same Demand No. 35, “Industries”, the allocation is Rs.7.50 crore. It should also not be forgotten that the allocation under the head of Industries falls under four demands. If we look at last year and the year before, it is only for the past two or three years that we have been following this method of placing separate demands of this kind. It was by combining all these demands that, in 1965-66, Rs.6.56 crore was allocated for industry, as Mr Gopal said. It is not right to take only one of the demands. That figure included the allocation of Rs.3.35 crore to the TANSI undertaking. Now, Rs.7 crore is being spent through TANSI separately, without being included in this amount. We should not forget that this is over and above the Rs.7.5 crore now allocated for industries in the State financial statement. Thus, if both are taken into account, instead of the Rs.6.56 crore spent on industrial development in 1965-66, it becomes clear that about Rs.15 crore is now being allocated for it.

Mr James spoke on this financial statement. He said that the Chief Minister had no right at all to present this financial statement here. Fortunately, instead of saying that because such an unqualified person had placed the financial statement before the House he could not speak on it, he showed affection towards me and expressed all his profound views on the financial statement. Though Mr James is not here now, I convey my thanks to him through Mr Ponnappa Nadar.

Mr James said one thing. He said: “The State Government had an income of Rs.194 crore in 1966-67. In 1973-74 the State Government's income is Rs.465 crore.” He then subtracted 194 from 465 and said that an additional Rs.271 crore in tax had therefore been imposed on the people. In other words, he treated the whole of revenue as tax: Rs.465 crore in tax now, Rs.194 crore in tax in 1966-67, so 465 minus 194 equals Rs.271 crore. That is an easy argument. They say, “7 plus 8 is 15.” We dispute it. They ask, “How can you dispute that 7 plus 8 is 15?”

What we say is, without doubt, 7 plus 8 is indeed 15. But if, in arriving at 7, you calculate 4 plus 2 as 7 and then say that 7 plus 8 is 15, that is what we are answering. In that sense, Mr James's statement that the State Government received Rs.194 crore in tax revenue in 1966-67 is wrong, and the claim that Rs.465 crore was received through taxes in 1973-74 is also wrong.
