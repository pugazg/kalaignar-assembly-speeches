Speech 12 — Date: 07.03.1973

**Hon. Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi:** Hon. Deputy Speaker, eighty-one members, including Mr Ponnappa Nadar, leader of the Congress Party, have expressed their views on the financial statement placed before this House. Of the 175 members of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, 38 members, and of the 58 members belonging to parties other than the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, 43 members, took part in this debate and explained many valuable views here in accordance with the policies of the parties they respectively uphold. Whether those views were favourable or adverse, in the belief that all of them will help the wheel of this administration turn properly, I first convey my thanks and appreciation to the members of all parties who expressed their views.

Ordinarily, the debate on the Governor's Address begins after a member of the ruling party moves a motion of thanks. A debate on the financial statement of the Chief Minister or Finance Minister ordinarily begins with the opposition speaking against it first. But, for the first time, I think, in this House, this debate began neither on behalf of the ruling party nor, conversely, on behalf of the opposition,

<!-- source-page: 192 -->

but on behalf of an allied party. I am very happy to see that, right at the outset, the debate began on the footing that there was no opposition to it. Hon. member Mr Wahab of the Muslim League supported this financial statement, offered his good wishes, and opened the debate.

The party leaders and some members who spoke here, including my dear friend and leader of the Communist Party of India, K.T.K. Thangamani, said that they had been prevented from expressing their views on this financial statement. When that was said on behalf of the Communist Party, I understood it. When Mr Hande spoke on behalf of the Swatantra Party, I understood it. When others, apart from Mrs Anandanayaki, spoke on behalf of the ruling Congress Party, I understood it. But when Mr Ponnappa Nadar of the old Congress Party and a few others spoke in that manner, I did not understand it. The Swatantra Party, the Communist Party and the ruling Congress Party here are not parties that have so far borne responsibility for governing Tamil Nadu. But the Congress Party of our Mr Ponnappa Nadar ruled Tamil Nadu for twenty years. Mr Ponnappa Nadar, leader of our Organisation Congress, was among those who supported the holding of the Assembly here after I had been locked up for sixty-two days in Palayamkottai prison. When our Hon. Education Minister, Navalar Nedunchezhiyan, had been convicted and was in Chennai prison, he asked for one day's leave so that he could come out, assume the chairmanship of the Public Accounts Committee which he headed, and conduct its proceedings. Mr Ponnappa Nadar supported the attitude of the then old Congress Government which refused that permission. When he spoke about this, I found it amusing.

Some Hon. members who spoke on this financial statement differed somewhat from an important point I had underlined. In particular, Mr Senti Sundarabharathi and some other members, through their differing views, rejected my suggestion that the Finance Commission should occupy the principal place in providing finance to the States. Mr Senti Sundarabharathi said here that a policy had been stated that allocation of funds to the States should be made through the Finance Commission, and that this did not appear to him to be a sound policy. My explanation to them is simply this: in the financial statement I said that the Finance Commission should occupy the principal place in arranging the means by which State Governments obtain finance from the Central Government. I did not say that the Finance Commission itself should calculate and recommend expenditure on Plan schemes, nor that the Planning Commission should have no role whatsoever in allocating funds.

<!-- source-page: 195 -->

Our view is that the Planning Commission may be entrusted with responsibility for giving assistance to the States for annual Plans in accordance with the principles of the Finance Commission. That is what I pointed out in this statement. It is not my argument that the Planning Commission should have no role of any kind in allocating funds.

At present, the State Government's share of Central taxes and the lump-sum grants given by the Finance Commission are provided in accordance with the Finance Commission's recommendations. We say that this should continue. The State Government has the right to spend these as it chooses. But, with regard to the amount which the Planning Commission allocates for State Plan expenditure—and I think it is useful for everyone to know this—of the amount allocated by the Planning Commission for State Plan expenditure, 70 per cent is given as loan assistance and only 30 per cent as grant. What I say is that, as far as this grant assistance is concerned, the Planning Commission should give it to the States in accordance with the general recommendations of the Finance Commission. This is also the view of the Central Administrative Reforms Commission. As for Plan loan assistance, it is the view of this Government that an All India lending institution should be established to provide loans for large specified Plan works, with representation for the States in that institution.

We have only said that assistance for Centrally Sponsored Schemes—works carried out by the State on behalf of the Central Government—and assistance for schemes initiated by the various departments of the Central Government but implemented in the State by the State Government should all be brought within the recommendations of the Finance Commission. I did not say that everything

The point he cited was this: in 1966-67, land revenue, irrigation tax, cess and agricultural income tax together yielded a total of Rs.840 lakh to this Government. That too must be taken into account.

Nor is that alone sufficient. In assessing land revenue, instead of looking only at how much is obtained each year, one must calculate the average obtained over each five-year period. That is because, in some years, remissions of tax or instalment arrangements may come into play. If we therefore take a five-year period and look at the average, for the five years ending with 1966-67—the period cited by Mr Kasiraman—the average annual revenue under land tax was Rs.8 crore. Similarly, for the five years ending with 1973-74, the average annual land-revenue income is Rs.6.3 crore. Thus it has declined by about Rs.2 crore. Because the basic land tax was abolished, ordinary farmers have benefited from these concessions. As time goes on, the amount received through this

It was by combining all these demands that, in 1965-66, Rs.6.56 crore was allocated for industry, as Mr Gopal said. It is not right to take only one of the demands. That figure included the allocation of Rs.3.35 crore to the TANSI undertaking. Now, Rs.7 crore is being spent through TANSI separately, without being included in this amount. We should not forget that this is over and above the Rs.7.5 crore now allocated for industries in the State financial statement. Thus, if both are taken into account, instead of the Rs.6.56 crore spent on industrial development in 1965-66, it becomes clear that about Rs.15 crore is now being allocated for it.

They say, “7 plus 8 is 15.” We dispute it. They ask, “How can you dispute that 7 plus 8 is 15?”

In that sense, Mr James's statement that the State Government received Rs.194 crore in tax revenue in 1966-67 is wrong, and the claim that Rs.465 crore was received through taxes in 1973-74 is also wrong. In every country, two things must be compared: (1) growth of national income, and (2) currency circulation—Money supply.

<!-- source-page: 208 -->

If we compare them, the Government of India's “Economic Survey 1972-73” states the position very clearly. Compared with 1968, growth in national income in 1968-69 rises by only 7 per cent. At the same time, money supply rises by 8.1 per cent. In 1969-70, compared with 1968-69, growth in national income rises by 7.3 per cent, while money supply rises by 10.8 per cent. But suddenly, and alarmingly, in 1971-72 national-income growth falls to 1.5, then stands hesitantly around two points. In contrast, money supply rises by 12.9 per cent. More or less the same situation continues in 1972-73.
