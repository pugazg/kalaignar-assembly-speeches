**Hon. Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi:** Hon. Speaker, seventy-seven members, including the leader of the Congress Party, have expressed their views here on the financial statement. At this stage I am greatly duty-bound to convey my thanks to those who spoke sharply in this debate, to those who spoke severely, to those who put forward sound and thoughtful ideas, and to those who offered clear and constructive ways by which the Government could correct itself if it were to slip anywhere from its duty.

When our Congress Party leader Mr Ponnappa Nadar spoke, we all noticed that half the speech was his own and half was that of Arignar Anna. Many views were expressed overall about the financial statement. A budget is connected with major questions such as increasing the pace of development, controlling prices and reducing unemployment.

If we look at that relationship under the two heads of reducing prices and removing unemployment, we must consider whether those matters are more closely connected with the budget prepared and issued by a State Government or with the budget prepared and issued by the Central Government. That is why, when our Mr K.T.K. Thangamani spoke, and when Mr Subramaniam of the ruling Congress spoke, they said that this looked like the annual report of a Panchayat Board, the annual report of a Panchayat Union, the annual report of a district development committee, even the annual report of a company.

I am not distressed by that. I am duty-bound to thank them for acknowledging the truth to this extent: that States now find themselves in a position where they can present their budgets only at the level of a Panchayat Union.

I wish to express my appreciation for their having indirectly pointed out that the States must certainly grow beyond the Panchayat Union level, remain truly as State Governments, and acquire the power and strength to present budgets worthy of State Governments.

Budgets presented at the State level can be framed only on the basis of the powers assigned to that State and the State's financial resources. A debate has been raised here: is a deficit budget good, or is a surplus budget good? That debate

was raised. From the nineteenth century to the beginning of the twentieth century—one may draw the line at 1930—the view was that preparing a deficit budget was dangerous and would raise prices. A contrary view was also expressed that a deficit budget could be good and that surplus budgets were not necessarily so desirable.

Instead of spending our time debating whether a surplus or deficit budget is good, we should examine a financial statement in the light of the circumstances in which the budget is prepared, the size of the deficit and the gap in that budget, the effort made to fill it, and the extent to which people at the bottom are affected by that effort.

Today, when a State Government prepares a deficit budget, it has to impose some taxes to meet the deficit. But when the Central Government prepares and issues a deficit budget, besides imposing taxes it can also permit a liberal expansion of money circulation to meet it. A State Government has neither the authority nor the circumstances that the Central Government has to permit such an expansion of money circulation.

Therefore, we must recognize that it is the Central Government's budget, through its ability to permit a liberal expansion of money circulation, that raises prices and creates unemployment, rather than say that merely because a few taxes are imposed to meet a deficit, the State Government's financial

As far as this Government is concerned, our taxation policy rests on the principle that taxes should be placed on those who can bear them and that new taxes should not be placed on those who cannot, and on the objective that taxes imposed should not allow prices or other matters to rise beyond control. On that basis we have imposed taxation amounting to Rs.10 crore to meet this deficit.

Through motor-tax reform, we expect to receive Rs.6½ crore.

For many years traders have been asking that multi-point sales tax be converted into single-point sales tax. By converting the multi-point tax on 59 commodities into single-point tax, by the revenue arising from that restructuring, by giving relief to traders doing business up to Rs.50,000, and by raising the surcharge from 5 per cent to 10 per cent for traders doing business above Rs.10 lakh—those who can bear it—we obtain Rs.3½ crore. In all, therefore, we have today made tax changes amounting to Rs.10 crore to meet this deficit. Even saying that we have “imposed taxes” is not quite correct; we have altered the tax structure.
