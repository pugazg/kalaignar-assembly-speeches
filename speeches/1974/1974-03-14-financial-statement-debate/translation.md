Instead of spending our time debating whether a surplus or deficit budget is good, we should examine a financial statement in the light of the circumstances in which the budget is prepared, the size of the deficit and the gap in that budget, the effort made to fill it, and the extent to which people at the bottom are affected by that effort.

Today, when a State Government prepares a deficit budget, it has to impose some taxes to meet the deficit. Mrs Anandanayaki said one thing: that “non-plan expenditure” was continuously increasing. She even said in English, referring to all Governments: ‘They must reduce the non-plan Expenditure’. What is “non-plan expenditure”? What is spent as Plan expenditure in the opening year of one Five-Year Plan becomes non-plan expenditure in the opening year of the next Five-Year Plan. That is “non-plan expenditure”. Suppose one thousand teachers are appointed in the opening year of a Five-Year Plan. At the beginning of the next Five-Year Plan, that becomes non-plan expenditure. If we accept the lady member's argument that it must be reduced, the Government would have to tell those thousand teachers: “We are going to reduce non-plan expenditure; therefore, you may go home.”

Similarly, suppose a scheme is undertaken in the opening year of a Five-Year Plan to build one hundred hospitals. Once those five years end and the next Five-Year Plan begins, all those hospitals fall under non-plan expenditure. They become non-plan expenditure. When that happens, we cannot close those hospitals merely in order to reduce non-plan expenditure, nor can we cut their costs and maintenance.
